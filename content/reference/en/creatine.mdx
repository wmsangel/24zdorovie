## What it is

Creatine is a compound your body partly makes itself (from amino acids) and partly gets from meat and fish. In muscle it's stored as phosphocreatine — a fast reserve for regenerating ATP during short, intense efforts. Supplementing raises that reserve, and that is where the effect on strength and power comes from.

It is the single most-researched sports supplement there is: hundreds of randomised trials and several position stands from scientific societies.

The key point: creatine works **alongside training**, not instead of it. It lets you do a little more quality work, and it's the work that drives the growth.

## Who it may help

- Strength and power sports, and anyone who trains regularly with weights.
- Older adults — to support muscle mass when combined with resistance training.
- Vegetarians and vegans: lower baseline muscle creatine means the response is often stronger.

## How to take it

- **Maintenance dose:** 3–5 g a day, consistently, at any time of day.
- **Loading (optional):** 20 g a day (4 doses of 5 g) for 5–7 days, then drop to 3–5 g. Speeds saturation to about a week instead of 3–4.
- **Form:** monohydrate — the proven standard. Pricey "newer" forms show no advantage.

<Callout type="science" title="Where the numbers come from">
  The doses and safety conclusions are taken from the International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand (ISSN, Kreider 2017), which pooled hundreds of studies.
</Callout>

## Food sources

Creatine is highest in red meat and fish — roughly 1–2 g per ~450 g of food. Getting a supplemental dose from food is hard and expensive, and cooking destroys some of it. That's why the supplement is used in sport.

## Cautions and common myths

<Callout type="warning" title="When to be careful">
  With kidney disease, clear creatine with your doctor first. A small, temporary gain in body weight from water held in muscle is normal — it isn't fat.
</Callout>

- **"It harms the kidneys."** Not supported in healthy people at recommended doses. Creatine raises creatinine on lab tests, but that's a marker, not a sign of damage.
- **"It causes hair loss."** Based on one small study of the hormone DHT that was never replicated. There's no established cause-and-effect.
- **"You need to cycle it."** There's no need to take breaks.

## Proven vs hyped

- **Proven:** strength, power and lean mass — when paired with training.
- **Hyped:** "fat burning", the superiority of pricey forms over monohydrate, harm to the heart — no evidence.
