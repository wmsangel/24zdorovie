## What it is

Coenzyme Q10 is a fat-soluble compound mitochondria use in the energy-production (ATP) chain, and which also acts as an antioxidant. The body synthesises its own; blood levels fall with age and on statins — which is where most of the marketing promises come from.

## Who it may help

Mostly this is a matter of medical prescription (for example, as an add-on in heart failure). For a healthy person 'for vigour' or 'against ageing', the evidence says a supplement doesn't help.

## How much to take

- **Dose:** studies used 100–200 mg/day, usually with fatty food (it's fat-soluble).
- **Form:** both ubiquinol and ubiquinone work; there's no clear absorption advantage despite the marketing.

<Callout type="science" title="Why there's so much noise around Q10">
  The logic "statins lower Q10, so top it up" sounds neat, but clinical trials have mostly failed to show it reduces muscle symptoms. An attractive hypothesis isn't yet a proven effect.
</Callout>

## Food sources

Organ meats (heart, liver), fatty fish, nuts, vegetable oils — but in small amounts. The body makes most of its Q10 itself, so "getting enough from food" isn't a concern.

## Cautions and myths

<Callout type="warning" title="When to check with a doctor">
  Q10 is usually well tolerated, but if you take prescription medication — especially blood thinners — clear it with your doctor first.
</Callout>

- **"Q10 restores energy and youth."** No evidence in healthy people.
- **"Essential for everyone on statins."** The effect on muscle pain is unproven.

## Proven vs hyped

- **Proven (moderately):** as an add-on to heart-failure therapy under medical supervision.
- **Hyped:** "energy", "anti-ageing", being a must on statins.
