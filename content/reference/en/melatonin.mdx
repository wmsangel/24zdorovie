## What it is

Melatonin is a hormone the pineal gland produces in darkness. It does not “put you to sleep”; it tells the brain that night has begun and helps align circadian rhythms. So it works as a timing signal (a chronobiotic), not as a sedative sleeping pill (NCCIH).

## When it actually helps

It is strongest where the internal clock is misaligned: jet lag, delayed sleep phase (late chronotype), and shift work. Per Cochrane, after crossing 5+ time zones, melatonin taken close to bedtime at the destination reduces jet lag — especially on eastward flights.

## When it barely helps

For ordinary chronic insomnia the effect is small. The AASM (2017) recommendation is **not** to use melatonin for sleep onset or sleep maintenance in adults; first-line treatment for insomnia is cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), not pills. That is a “weak” recommendation by evidence quality, but the direction is clear.

## Dose and timing

Working doses are low: 0.5–3 mg. Per Cochrane, for jet lag doses of 0.5–5 mg are comparable in effect, and above 5 mg is not more effective. What matters most is **when** you take it: for jet lag, close to bedtime at the destination; taking it too early in the day can worsen jet lag.

The takeaway: melatonin is stronger where you need to shift the clock, not where you want to “switch the brain off” for the night.

## How to take it

A low dose (0.5–3 mg) half an hour to an hour before target bedtime; for jet lag — follow the scheme in the jet-lag article (close to bedtime at the destination). Do not raise the dose “to be sure.” Do not turn melatonin into an every-night habit for ordinary insomnia — start with sleep hygiene and CBT-I.

## Cautions and myths

<Callout type="warning" title="When to be careful">
  For children, pregnancy and breastfeeding — only with a clinician. Use caution with epilepsy and anticoagulants (warfarin); do not combine with alcohol. Supplement forms can have inaccurate label dosing (NCCIH). Short courses are usually well tolerated (possible daytime sleepiness, headache, dizziness), but long-term safety is poorly studied.
</Callout>

- **“Melatonin is a safe sleeping pill.”** It is not a sleeping pill — it is a hormone signal that “it is night now.” Dose and timing matter; “every night for insomnia” is not the niche where it is proven.
