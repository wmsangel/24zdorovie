This sheet-pan salmon and quinoa bowl takes 30 minutes start to finish and delivers about 38 g of protein, 2.5 g of omega-3s (EPA plus DHA) and 10 g of fiber per serving. That is roughly five times the daily omega-3 reference intake and about a third of a typical protein target, from a dinner with no marinating and no sauce to make. The quinoa simmers while the fish and vegetables share one pan.

## Why salmon and quinoa

Oily fish is the only food that supplies EPA and DHA ready-made. The plant alternative — ALA from flax, chia and walnuts — converts to EPA at only a few percent and to DHA at well under one percent. That is why the FDA and EPA advice is blunt about it: two to three servings of fish a week, and salmon sits in the "Best Choices" category, high in omega-3s and low in mercury.

According to USDA figures, 100 g of raw farmed Atlantic salmon contains roughly 1.1 g of DHA and 0.86 g of EPA. A 4 1/2 oz fillet therefore delivers around 2.5 g against a reference intake of 250–500 mg per day. One dinner covers several days at once.

Quinoa is not just filler underneath. Unlike rice, it contains all nine essential amino acids and adds nearly 6 g of protein and 3 g of fiber per serving. It also has a purely practical advantage: 15 minutes of simmering is exactly how long the oven needs.

<Callout type="science" title="How much omega-3 you actually need">
  The reference intake for a healthy adult is 250–500 mg of combined EPA and DHA per day. It is not a daily threshold you have to hit — the body stores these fats, so two servings of oily fish a week works the same as a capsule every morning. Supplements make sense mainly if fish is essentially absent from your diet.
</Callout>

## What you need for two servings

- **2 skin-on salmon fillets, 4 1/2 oz (130 g) each.** Leave the skin on while roasting: it holds moisture in. Peel it off at the table if you prefer
- **1/2 cup (85 g) uncooked quinoa.** Yields about 1 1/2 cups cooked. White, red or a tricolor mix — the difference is mostly visual
- **3 cups (200 g) broccoli florets.** Cut them to a similar size or the small ones scorch
- **1 large red bell pepper (150 g).** Wide strips, about 3/4 inch
- **2 packed cups (60 g) baby spinach.** Raw, folded into the hot quinoa at the end
- **1/2 small avocado (60 g).** Creaminess in place of a dressing
- **2 tsp (10 g) olive oil.** Half for the vegetables, half for the fish
- **1 tbsp (10 g) sesame seeds.** Toasted, for crunch
- **Lemon, garlic and oregano.** Acid and garlic do the work of a sauce; nothing else is needed

## The method, step by step

1. **Start — 2 minutes.** Heat the oven to 400°F (200°C). Rinse the quinoa under cold running water for 30 seconds. This washes off the saponins that make unrinsed quinoa taste faintly soapy.
2. **Quinoa — 15 minutes, in parallel.** Simmer it covered in 1 cup (240 ml) of salted water — a 1:2 ratio by volume — over the lowest heat for 15 minutes. Take it off the heat and let it stand for 5 minutes with the lid on. The steam finishes the job and the grains separate.
3. **Vegetables — 8 minutes.** Toss the broccoli and pepper with half the oil, salt, pepper and oregano. Spread them out on a sheet pan and roast for 8 minutes on their own.
4. **Fish — 12 minutes.** Pat the fillets completely dry with paper towel. This step is not optional: a wet surface steams instead of roasting. Season, rub with the remaining oil and crushed garlic, then push the vegetables to the edges of the pan, set the fillets skin-side down in the middle, and roast for 12 minutes more.
5. **Assembly — 3 minutes.** While the pan is in the oven, slice the avocado and toast the sesame seeds in a dry skillet. Divide the quinoa between two deep bowls and fold in the spinach — the hot grain wilts it perfectly. Add the vegetables, the salmon and the avocado, scatter the sesame, and squeeze over the lemon.

<Callout type="tip" title="How to pick good salmon">
  Fresh fillets should spring back when pressed — a dent that stays is a bad sign. Look for even orange-pink flesh with no gray or brown edges and thin, distinct white fat lines. It should smell of the sea, not of fish. For frozen, choose vacuum-sealed packs with no ice crystals inside the bag; large crystals mean it thawed and refroze. Farmed salmon is fattier and higher in omega-3s per ounce, wild is leaner and slightly higher in protein — either works here.
</Callout>

About half the fat comes from the fish itself, and it is the unsaturated kind you are cooking this dish for in the first place. Fiber lands near 10 g per serving — broccoli, quinoa, avocado and pepper together supply about a third of the daily target, which is unusual for a dinner.

The ratio is worth noting too: 38 g of protein at 620 calories means roughly a quarter of the energy comes from protein. In a plate of pasta with tomato sauce that share is closer to 13%. That is why this bowl holds you for hours even though the portion does not look enormous.

<Callout type="warning" title="If you swap the grain">
  Bulgur (12 minutes), red lentils (15 minutes) and quick-cook pearl barley all slot into the timing unchanged. Standard brown rice needs 35–40 minutes, so it has to go on before the oven does. And whatever else you do, rinse the quinoa: the saponin coating is genuinely bitter and no amount of lemon will hide it.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

**Putting the fish in with the vegetables.** Broccoli needs about 20 minutes, salmon needs 12. Load the pan all at once and the fish is dry by the time the florets are tender. The eight-minute head start is the whole trick.

**Skipping the paper towel.** A damp fillet steams in its own moisture and comes out pale and watery instead of lightly browned.

**Overcooking.** Salmon is done in 10–12 minutes at 400°F and keeps cooking for a couple of minutes out of the oven. Aim for 125°F (52°C) at the center, or large moist flakes when you press with a fork.

**Not rinsing the quinoa.** The saponins on the outside of the grain taste soapy and bitter, and nothing you add afterward will mask it.

**Too much water in the quinoa.** A 1:2 ratio by volume plus five minutes of covered resting is all it takes. Extra water turns the grain to porridge.
