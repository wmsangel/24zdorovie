Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is a structured psychotherapy that works with how thoughts, emotions and behavior are linked. Its key idea is simple: our reactions are shaped largely not by events themselves but by how we interpret them — and by changing thoughts and actions we can change how we feel. It is the most studied form of psychotherapy, with a strong evidence base for anxiety, depression, panic and insomnia. Here is how it works and what you can try on your own.

## What CBT is built on

At its core is the observation that thoughts, emotions and behavior form a closed loop. The same situation produces different feelings depending on how it is interpreted. A missed call can be read as "they don't care about me" — and upset you — or as "they're probably busy" — and go unnoticed. The body's reaction and behavior follow the interpretation.

CBT teaches you to notice this mechanism and intervene at two points: **in thoughts** (spotting distortions and testing them against reality) and **in behavior** (gradually re-approaching what you avoid). It is the combination of the two that makes the method work.

<Callout type="science" title="Why it is called the gold standard">
  CBT is the most researched form of psychotherapy: hundreds of randomised trials and dozens of meta-analyses. For many anxiety and depressive disorders it is comparable in effectiveness to medication, and its results more often persist after the course ends, because the person takes the skills with them.
</Callout>

## What it is not

The main misunderstanding is that CBT teaches you to "think positive". It does not. The method does not replace anxious thoughts with pleasant slogans; it helps you see where a thought is distorted and test it against the facts. Sometimes the honest conclusion is "yes, there is a risk, but I can cope", not "everything will be wonderful". The goal is accuracy and flexibility of view, not forced optimism.

Typical distortions CBT helps you notice: catastrophising ("the worst will happen"), mind-reading ("everyone thinks I'm stupid"), overgeneralising ("I never get it right"), black-and-white thinking.

## What you can try yourself

For mild symptoms, it is reasonable to start with self-help — it has an evidence base:

- **Thought record** — write down the situation, the automatic thought, the emotion, and then a more balanced alternative.
- **Behavioral activation** — deliberately re-introduce activities that bring meaning and pleasure, even when you "don't feel like it".
- **CBT workbooks and apps** — structure these techniques.

<Callout type="warning" title="When you need a professional">
  Self-help is good for mild symptoms. But with marked anxiety, depression, panic, obsessions or thoughts that life is not worth living, you need to work with a professional. Some techniques — such as exposure for panic and OCD — are more effective and safer done under a therapist's guidance.
</Callout>

CBT is not magic or the "power of positivity" but a skill of seeing your thoughts more clearly and acting despite discomfort. Like any skill, it is trained — which is exactly why it works.
