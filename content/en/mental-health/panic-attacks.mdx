A panic attack is a sudden surge of intense fear with vivid physical symptoms: a racing heart, breathlessness, dizziness, trembling, numbness. It peaks in about 10 minutes and feels like a genuine catastrophe, even a fear of dying — yet it is not itself life-threatening. Understanding how the mechanism works already loosens its grip. Here is why panic scares you so much, how to break its vicious cycle, and what actually treats it.

## What happens in the body

Panic is based on the ancient "fight or flight" response. The brain decides there is a threat and triggers a surge of adrenaline: the heart beats faster, breathing quickens, blood shifts to the muscles. All of this is useful when real danger is ahead. The problem with a panic attack is that the response fires without an external threat — a "false alarm" of the safety system.

Hence the deceptiveness of the symptoms. The brain may read a racing heart and chest tightness as "I'm having a heart attack", and dizziness as "I'm passing out or going mad". These interpretations frighten you even more — and pump out more adrenaline. This is how the cycle closes.

<Callout type="science" title="The vicious cycle of panic">
  Body sensation → frightening interpretation ("this is dangerous") → rising anxiety → stronger sensations → an even more frightening interpretation. The essence of a panic attack lies in this loop, not in the sensations themselves. Breaking the loop means changing the interpretation — and treatment is built on exactly that.
</Callout>

## Attack or disorder

A single panic attack amid heavy stress, lack of sleep or caffeine can happen to many — it is not a diagnosis. **Panic disorder** is when:

- attacks recur;
- a persistent fear of new attacks develops;
- the person starts avoiding places and situations where panic has already happened.

It is this avoidance that gradually narrows life and entrenches the problem — and it is exactly what responds well to treatment.

## What to do during an attack

The main principle is paradoxical: **do not fight it**. Trying to forcibly "stop" panic is read by the brain as confirmation of a threat and strengthens it. The opposite works — letting the wave pass, remembering that it peaks and subsides on its own.

## How it is treated

The good news: panic disorder is one of the most treatable conditions. The first line is **cognitive behavioral therapy**. It has a particularly effective element — interoceptive exposure: under a therapist's guidance, the person deliberately brings on harmless bodily sensations (mild dizziness, for example) so the brain relearns not to treat them as dangerous. This directly breaks the vicious cycle.

For frequent, severe attacks a doctor may add medication. Caffeine, lack of sleep and alcohol increase the tendency to panic, so cutting them back is a sensible support to therapy.

<Callout type="warning" title="When you must see a doctor">
  A first-ever episode with chest pain, severe breathlessness or fainting should be shown to a doctor to rule out heart problems and other causes: the symptoms of panic and of some illnesses overlap. And if attacks recur, that is a reason to seek help — not because it is dangerous, but because it is treatable.
</Callout>

Panic frightens precisely because it feels lethally dangerous while not being so. Understanding the mechanism, self-help skills and, if needed, therapy return control — and for most people the attacks recede.
