Depression isn't "a bad mood" or weakness of character — it's a medical condition: a persistently low mood and/or loss of interest nearly every day for **two weeks or more**, along with changes in sleep, appetite, energy and concentration that get in the way of working and living. The key thing to know up front: depression responds well to treatment — therapy, and medication when needed — and the sooner you reach out, the sooner it eases. If you have thoughts of not wanting to live, that's a reason to get help now, not to wait.

## What depression is, and how it differs from sadness

Sadness or feeling down after a loss or setback is a normal reaction. It's tied to a specific cause, fades with time, and doesn't take away your ability to enjoy good things. **Major depressive disorder** is different: the mood is low, or interest is gone, almost constantly, regardless of events, and it lasts for weeks.

By the DSM-5 criteria, a depressive episode is diagnosed when, for **two weeks or more**, at least five of the symptoms below are present nearly every day — and at least one of the first two (low mood or loss of interest and pleasure) must be among them.

A note of caution: having a few of these for a couple of days is not a diagnosis. A clinician makes the diagnosis, weighing duration, the number of symptoms, and how much they disrupt life.

<Callout type="danger" title="When to get help right now">
  If you have thoughts of not wanting to live, of self-harm, or specific plans — don't stay alone with it. Call your local emergency services or a suicide-prevention lifeline (in the US, call or text **988**), and reach out to people close to you. This is not "weakness" and not forever — an acute state can be relieved, and things get better after it.

## A quick self-check: the PHQ-9 scale

PHQ-9 is a short, validated questionnaire that clinicians worldwide use to gauge the severity of depressive symptoms over the past two weeks. It is **not a diagnosis** — it's a guide: it helps you see how serious things are and whether to see a professional. The calculation runs right in your browser; your answers are never sent anywhere.

## Why depression happens

Depression comes from a combination of factors, not from anyone's fault. **Genetic predisposition**, chronic or severe stress, traumatic events, physical illnesses and some medications, disrupted sleep, and biological differences in how the brain works all play a part. That's why "just pull yourself together" doesn't work — it's no more a matter of willpower than type 2 diabetes is.

## What actually helps

The good news: depression is one of the most studied and most treatable conditions there is. There are two main approaches that work, and they're often combined.

**Psychotherapy.** Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) and interpersonal therapy (IPT) have the strongest evidence base. CBT teaches you to notice and change distorted thoughts and to rebuild activity step by step — and the effect lasts after the course ends.

**Antidepressants.** In moderate to severe depression they help stabilise your state. A doctor prescribes them; the effect builds gradually (the first change usually after 2–4 weeks), and the course runs for months to lower the relapse risk. They aren't addictive, but they should be tapered off gradually and supervised.

**Lifestyle is an add-on, not a replacement.** Regular movement, decent sleep and real-life connection reliably support mood. But in moderate to severe depression they strengthen the main treatment rather than replace it.

## What not to do

- **"Just snap out of it" and self-blame.** Depression doesn't yield to willpower; such advice deepens guilt.
- **Alcohol "to relax".** It dulls things briefly but worsens sleep and deepens depression.
- **Supplements and "natural" remedies instead of treatment.** Most have no solid evidence; losing time in depression is dangerous.
- **Stopping antidepressants on your own.** Abrupt stopping causes discontinuation symptoms — taper with a doctor.

## When you must see a doctor

See a professional (a therapist, a psychiatrist, or your primary-care doctor to start) if symptoms last **two weeks or more** and get in the way of work, study or relationships; if what used to help no longer does; or if you have thoughts of not wanting to live (then — right away). Depression and anxiety often go together, so it's worth checking your anxiety level too.
