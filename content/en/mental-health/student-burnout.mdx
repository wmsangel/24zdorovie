Student burnout is the burnout pattern applied to study: not one bad week, but a sustained state along three axes — **exhaustion** from coursework, **cynicism** toward your studies, and a **reduced sense of academic efficacy**. It's well-documented in research, measured with student-specific scales, and — crucially — it's the opposite of laziness: it usually follows a long stretch of pushing hard, not of doing nothing.

## The three dimensions, in academic terms

The model built for the workplace was adapted to education decades ago. The student version — measured by the Maslach Burnout Inventory–Student Survey and the School Burnout Inventory — keeps the same three axes:

**Exhaustion** is the energy dimension: nothing left for coursework, sleep that no longer restores, dread before opening a textbook. It's the first thing students notice and the one that responds fastest to a lighter load.

**Cynicism** is the attitude dimension: your degree stops feeling meaningful, lectures start to grate, you do the minimum to get by. This is the dimension that predicts dropping out, and rest barely touches it — because its cause is usually structural, not a lack of sleep.

**Reduced academic efficacy** is the competence dimension: the sense that you handle things worse than you used to, that easy assignments have become slow, that finishing brings no satisfaction. It tends to appear last and to recover last.

The practical use of this list is diagnostic. If your cynicism is the high dimension, run down the rows and ask which mismatch is worst — students can usually name it at once, and it's more often lost meaning or lost control than raw workload.

## Burnout, exam stress and depression are not the same

These get confused, and the difference changes what you should do.

**Exam stress** is acute and self-limiting: it climbs toward a deadline and drops once the exam is over. That's a normal, even useful, response.

**Burnout** is chronic: it builds over a term or more, and a single break lowers the exhaustion but the state returns within weeks if nothing about the conditions changed.

**Depression** touches every area of life, not just study, and carries its own criteria. A review of 92 studies concluded the boundary between burnout and depression is conceptually fragile — which is exactly why a self-test can't settle it.

<Callout type="warning" title="When to stop self-assessing and talk to someone">
  If low mood, loss of interest and low energy have spread beyond your studies, lasted more than two weeks, or are interfering with sleep and eating, treat that as a reason to contact your university's health or counselling service — free and confidential at most institutions. Seek help immediately if you have thoughts that life is not worth living.
</Callout>

## Where to start if this sounds familiar

The evidence on burnout is consistent on one point: interventions that change the conditions work better than ones aimed only at the individual. As a student you have less control than an employer, but more than it feels like in the moment.

<KeyPoints title="What tends to help" items={[
  "Protect recovery: fixed sleep, and time that is genuinely not studying",
  "Cut the load where you can — drop, defer or renegotiate a deadline",
  "Rebuild meaning: reconnect the work to a goal, or change the goal",
  "Study with others; isolation deepens cynicism",
  "Move: regular activity is one of the best-evidenced buffers for mood",
  "Use the support that exists — tutors, advisers, counselling"
]} />

Start with the dimension that's highest. High exhaustion with intact interest usually means volume, and reducing load still works. High cynicism usually means something structural — meaning, fairness, control — and rest alone won't move it.
