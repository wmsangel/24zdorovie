Trans fat and saturated fat get filed together as "the bad fats", and that grouping is the reason most people misjudge both. They are not two points on the same scale. Saturated fat is a normal component of food whose effect depends almost entirely on what you eat instead of it. Industrial trans fat is a manufacturing artefact that harms you at doses small enough to fit in a biscuit, and the global public health position is not to limit it but to remove it.

## The chemistry, in one paragraph

A fatty acid is a chain of carbon atoms. In a **saturated** fatty acid every carbon holds as much hydrogen as it can and there are no double bonds, so the chain is straight, the molecules stack neatly, and the fat is solid at room temperature. That is butter, lard and coconut oil.

An **unsaturated** fatty acid has one or more double bonds. In nature those bonds are almost always in the *cis* configuration, with both hydrogens on the same side, which puts a kink in the chain. Kinked chains cannot pack tightly, so the fat is liquid. That is olive oil and sunflower oil.

A **trans** fatty acid also has a double bond — but with the hydrogens on opposite sides, which leaves the chain straight. So it is chemically unsaturated and physically behaves like a saturated fat: solid, stable, long shelf life. That combination is exactly what made partial hydrogenation commercially attractive in the twentieth century, and it is also why the molecule slots into human metabolism in ways evolution never prepared for.

The row that decides the comparison is the second one. Both fats raise LDL, so an argument that stops at LDL makes them look similar. Saturated fat, however, raises HDL a little at the same time, which partly offsets its effect on the total-to-HDL ratio. Trans fat pushes HDL down. The Mensink meta-analysis of 60 controlled feeding trials quantified this: per unit of energy, trans fat worsens the total-cholesterol-to-HDL ratio roughly twice as much as saturated fat does.

## The dose is where the gap becomes obvious

Comparing directions of effect understates the difference. Compare magnitudes and the two stop looking like members of the same category.

Mozaffarian and colleagues, reviewing the evidence in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 2006, put the figure at roughly a **23% increase in coronary heart disease risk for each 2% of daily energy from trans fat**. On a 2,000 kcal diet, 2% of energy is about 4.4 grams. That is a single doughnut in the pre-ban era, or a portion of fries fried in partially hydrogenated oil.

Saturated fat does not behave like that. Its risk relationships are shallow enough that the entire scientific argument is about the replacement nutrient rather than the fat itself. Li and colleagues, following 127,000 people for 24 to 30 years, found that replacing 5% of energy from saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat was associated with 25% lower coronary risk; replacing the same 5% with refined carbohydrate was associated with essentially no change.

  It does not mean a single biscuit will hurt you. It means that across the studied range there is no intake of industrial trans fat below which the risk curve flattens out — so regulators cannot name a threshold to permit. That is a statement about the shape of the evidence, and it is not true of any other dietary fat.

## Saturated fat, without the simplification

The public conversation about saturated fat swings between "it causes heart disease" and "it was exonerated", and neither is what the evidence says.

The 2020 Cochrane review, pooling 15 randomised trials with 56,675 participants, found that reducing saturated fat cut cardiovascular events by roughly 17%. The effect was concentrated in the trials that replaced it with polyunsaturated vegetable oils. Trials that replaced it with carbohydrate showed no benefit.

So saturated fat is not a poison, and it is not neutral either. It is a fat whose effect is defined by its alternative. Cutting butter and eating white bread instead achieves nothing measurable. Cutting butter and using olive or rapeseed oil instead achieves something real.

## The ruminant exception

Not all trans fat is manufactured. Bacteria in the stomachs of cows, sheep and goats produce trans fatty acids naturally — mainly vaccenic acid and conjugated linoleic acid — and these end up in milk, butter and beef, where they make up roughly 2 to 5% of the fat.

Studies have not found the same cardiovascular risk for ruminant trans fats at usual intakes. There are two honest readings of that, and they are not mutually exclusive. One is that the mixture genuinely differs — the specific isomers are not the same as the ones partial hydrogenation produces. The other is that almost nobody consumes enough of them for a signal to appear: typical intake is a fraction of what industrial trans fat intake used to be. Both readings lead to the same practical conclusion, which is that dairy fat is a saturated fat question, not a trans fat one.

## Where trans fat still hides

Denmark set the first national limit in 2003. The US revoked the "generally recognised as safe" status of partially hydrogenated oils in 2015, with compliance completed by 2021. The WHO's REPLACE package has driven regulation covering most of the world's population. In countries with enforced limits, industrial trans fat has largely left the food supply.

Largely is not entirely, and two gaps are worth knowing about.

**The rounding rule.** In several jurisdictions a product may declare "0 g trans fat" if it contains less than 0.5 g per serving. Serving sizes are small, and a few of them across a day quietly add up. The fix is to read the ingredient list instead: **partially hydrogenated oil** is the phrase that matters. Fully hydrogenated oil is chemically different and contains essentially no trans fat.

**Uneven enforcement.** Regulation is not universal, and imported baked goods, non-dairy creamers, filled biscuits, some margarines and repeatedly reused deep-frying oil remain the categories where trans fat turns up in countries without enforced limits.

## What to actually do

For **trans fat**: treat it as something to reach zero on rather than to budget. Check ingredient lists for partially hydrogenated oil, and be sceptical of long-shelf-life baked goods with a suspiciously creamy filling. There is no benefit being traded away here — trans fat has no nutritional role at all.

For **saturated fat**: keep it under 10% of energy, which is about 22 g on a 2,000 kcal diet, and put your attention on the replacement rather than the restriction. Swapping butter for olive oil, fatty processed meat for fish or legumes, and cream for yoghurt does the work. Swapping fat for sugar does not.

And keep the proportions right. A person eating no industrial trans fat and 12% of energy from saturated fat has a small, fixable issue. A person eating 3% of energy from trans fat has a large one, regardless of everything else on their plate.
