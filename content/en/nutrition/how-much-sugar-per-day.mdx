The working ceiling for an adult is about 50 g of added sugar a day — 10 percent of energy on a 2,000-calorie diet, or roughly 12 teaspoons. Cutting to 25 g, about 5 percent of energy, buys further benefit, and cardiology guidance treats that lower figure as the actual target: up to 25 g a day for women and 36 g for men. What you count is only the added sugar, not every sugar in your food — the sugar inside whole fruit, vegetables, and plain milk is not part of the budget.

A useful conversion to keep in your head: one teaspoon of sugar is 4 g and 16 calories. So the WHO ceiling is roughly 200 calories a day of pure sugar, and the AHA target for women is only 100.

Note that the thresholds are expressed as a share of energy, not as fixed grams. Someone eating 1,500 calories a day has a ceiling closer to 37 g than 50 g. That matters most for people trying to lose weight: the smaller your calorie budget, the less of it you can afford to spend on something that supplies no nutrients at all.

<Callout type="science" title="Why Europe declined to name a number">
  In 2022, EFSA's nutrition panel set out to establish a tolerable upper intake level for dietary sugars and could not do it. The obstacle was not thin evidence — it was that across the entire observed range of intake, no threshold appeared below which risk stopped rising. The panel's conclusion came without a figure: intake of added and free sugars should be "as low as possible" within a nutritionally adequate diet. The practical reading is that 50 g is a sensible cap, not a certified-safe dose.
</Callout>

## What actually counts as "added"

The vocabulary here is genuinely confusing, and the confusion is convenient for manufacturers.

**Added sugar** is any sugar put into a food during processing, cooking, or at the table: granulated sugar, high-fructose corn syrup, maltodextrin, molasses, honey, maple syrup, fruit juice concentrate. It was not part of the original plant or milk. Someone put it there.

**Free sugars** is the WHO's slightly broader term. It covers added sugar plus the sugars naturally present in honey, syrups, and fruit juices, including freshly squeezed ones. The logic is straightforward: once sugar has been freed from the cell structure of the fruit, your body meets it the same way it meets a spoonful of table sugar.

**Naturally occurring sugars** sit inside intact foods — fructose in an apple, lactose in milk and plain yogurt, sugars in carrots and beets. These are not counted, and no major guideline places a limit on them.

### Why whole fruit gets a pass and juice does not

A medium apple contains about 19 g of sugar. An 8 oz glass of apple juice contains about 24 g. The numbers are close; the behavior is not.

In the apple, sugar is locked inside cell walls and packaged with roughly 4 g of fiber that slows absorption. You have to chew it, it takes up room in your stomach, and it registers as food. The juice is the pressed sugar of three or four apples, drinkable in under a minute, with almost no effect on appetite.

That is why the WHO puts juice in the free-sugars category and whole fruit outside it. "No sugar added" on a juice carton is legally accurate and physiologically meaningless.

<Callout type="info" title="Dried fruit and smoothies">
  Dried fruit is whole fruit with the water removed: the fiber survives, but the sugar is concentrated, and 100 g of dates goes down far more easily than the equivalent 300 g of grapes. It is not classed as free sugar, but a 30–40 g serving is a reasonable stopping point. Smoothies land in between — blending keeps the fiber, yet sugar still arrives faster than it would from a plate of fruit.
</Callout>

## Why the limits exist

### Weight

This is where the case is often overstated, so it is worth being precise. The systematic review and meta-analysis by Te Morenga and colleagues (BMJ, 2013) handled the question carefully: in adults eating freely, reducing sugar intake produced an average weight loss of about 0.8 kg, and increasing it produced a gain of about 0.75 kg. But when sugar was swapped for an equal number of calories from other carbohydrates, body weight did not change at all.

The conclusion is unglamorous and frequently mangled. Sugar has no special power to become body fat. What it has is calorie density with almost no satiety, especially in liquid form. The same review found that in long-term cohort studies, the highest intakes of sugar-sweetened beverages were associated with substantially higher odds of obesity.

### Teeth

This is the least controversial part of the whole subject. The systematic review by Moynihan and Kelly, commissioned specifically to inform the WHO guideline, found that dental caries is lower when free sugars stay under 10 percent of energy, and lower still under 5 percent. That analysis is the direct basis for the WHO's conditional 5 percent recommendation.

For teeth, frequency matters more than daily total. Five separate teaspoons spread across the day are worse than five at once, because each exposure triggers roughly half an hour of acid on the enamel surface.

### Heart and metabolism

The prospective analysis by Yang and colleagues (JAMA Internal Medicine, 2014) followed US adults and found a clear dose-response relationship: people getting 25 percent or more of their calories from added sugar had close to triple the risk of cardiovascular death compared with those staying under 10 percent. The association held after adjustment for body mass index, physical activity, and overall diet quality.

This is observational evidence and does not prove causation on its own. The mechanisms, though, are well described: excess fructose is processed by the liver, and a chronic surplus promotes fat accumulation there along with rising blood triglycerides. EFSA's 2022 review graded the certainty of a causal link between sugars and obesity and dyslipidaemia as moderate, and the link with fatty liver disease and type 2 diabetes as low.

<Callout type="warning" title="Who should aim lower than 10 percent">
  Insulin resistance, prediabetes, type 2 diabetes, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, elevated triglycerides, and gout are all reasons to target the 5 percent figure and drop sugar-sweetened drinks entirely. Children under two are a separate case: added sugar is not recommended for them at all, largely because early exposure shapes taste preferences for years.
</Callout>

## Where it hides

The standard assumption is that sugar comes from candy and cake. In practice, someone who "hardly eats sweets" can clear the daily ceiling on drinks, sauces, and breakfast alone.

### Reading the label

US Nutrition Facts panels have made this unusually easy: since the 2016 label update, "Includes Xg Added Sugars" appears as its own line, with a percent Daily Value based on 50 g. Most other markets, including the EU and the Eurasian Economic Union, still print only "of which sugars," which combines natural and added sugar and needs interpreting.

Either way, four habits cover it:

1. **Read the ingredient list, not just the panel.** Ingredients are listed by weight. If a sugar appears in the first three, the product is a sweet, whatever it is marketed as.
2. **Learn the aliases.** Sucrose, glucose, fructose, dextrose, maltose, corn syrup, high-fructose corn syrup, invert syrup, molasses, maltodextrin, fruit juice concentrate, malt extract, honey, agave nectar. Splitting sugar across several named types pushes each one further down the list.
3. **Subtract the natural sugar when there is no added-sugar line.** Plain milk and unsweetened yogurt run about 4.7 g of lactose per 100 g. If a yogurt label says 12 g of sugars, roughly 7 g of that was added.
4. **Treat "no sugar added" as marketing.** It means no sucrose was poured in. It does not rule out concentrates, syrups, or sweeteners — and on juice it tells you nothing useful at all.

<Callout type="tip" title="The three-label rule">
  Do not try to audit your entire pantry. Read the ingredients on three things you buy every single week — usually a yogurt, a cereal, and a sauce. Those three items typically account for most of the sugar you did not know you were eating, and swapping them solves half the problem without restricting anything.
</Callout>

## Cutting back without misery

Going cold turkey rarely holds. Taste receptors adapt gradually, and trying to switch to unsweetened everything overnight usually ends in a rebound. Four steps work better.

**Start with what you drink.** Beverages are both the largest and the easiest source. Soda, juice, sweet tea, and syrup-heavy coffee drinks come out of a diet almost painlessly, because they never made you feel full and they replace no actual food. For a lot of people this single change does the entire job.

**Step the sweetness down rather than off.** If you take two sugars in your coffee, go to one and a half for two weeks, then to one. The perception threshold for sweetness shifts within weeks, and after a month the original version tastes cloying.

**Buy the unsweetened base and add your own.** Plain yogurt with a handful of berries carries a fraction of the sugar of the flavored cup, and oatmeal with a sliced banana beats any instant packet. You end up controlling the amount instead of the manufacturer.

**Do not cut sugar in isolation.** Most lapses come from hunger, not weak willpower. Adequate protein and fiber blunt sugar cravings noticeably, because they steady blood glucose and extend satiety.

## What about sweeteners

The WHO's position surprises people: it recommends reducing free sugars without switching to non-sugar sweeteners. In 2023 the organization issued a separate conditional recommendation against using sweeteners for weight control, on the grounds that trials show no long-term benefit for body weight or metabolic markers.

That is not a claim that sweeteners are dangerous at permitted intakes. The point is narrower — replacing soda with its zero-sugar version does not accomplish the thing that actually matters, which is unhooking your palate from a constant sweet signal. As a bridge for a couple of months it is reasonable. As a permanent strategy it is questionable.

The same skepticism applies to "better" sugars. Brown sugar, raw cane sugar, coconut sugar, honey, agave, and maple syrup are all free sugars under the WHO definition. They do contain trace minerals, in quantities so small that you would have to eat a self-defeating amount of sugar to obtain a meaningful dose.

## What to do in practice

1. Work out your own ceiling: 10 percent of your daily calories at most, 5 percent as a target. On 2,000 calories that is 50 g and 25 g.
2. For three days, log only what you drink. Not meals — drinks. That is where the sugar usually is.
3. Read the ingredient list on three products you buy every week and find the sugar in them.
4. Replace one sweetened drink a day with water, tea, or unsweetened coffee. That alone is 30–40 g.
5. Do not extend the restriction to whole fruit, vegetables, or plain dairy. Their sugar is not in the budget and no guideline limits it.

The point of all this is not to treat sugar as poison. A spoonful in your coffee is not wrecking your health, and no food becomes toxic simply because sucrose is present. The problem is background exposure: in an ordinary supermarket diet, sugar is in nearly everything, and 50 g accumulates without a single dessert. Which is why the thing that works is not willpower — it is paying attention to labels and to what is in your glass.
