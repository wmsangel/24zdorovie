Plant and animal protein differ in two things: amino acid profile and digestibility. Animal proteins are "complete" and better absorbed; many plant proteins are lower in specific amino acids and digest slightly less well. But this difference is overcome by variety and a small increase in amount — and for overall health, a shift toward plant protein is actually linked to benefits. Here it is without the extremes of "meat is poison" or "plants do not fill you up".

## Two real differences

**Amino acid profile.** Protein is made of amino acids, nine of which are essential — you must get them from food. Animal proteins contain them all in favourable proportions, which is why they are called "complete". Many plant proteins are lower in specific amino acids: legumes in methionine, grains in lysine. So a single plant food as your main protein source is not the best idea.

**Digestibility.** Animal protein is on average digested and absorbed more fully. Plant protein is "packed" in cell walls and fiber, and some passes through. Hence the practical takeaway: on a plant-based diet, the total protein target is raised by about 10%.

<Callout type="science" title="The 'combine in one meal' myth">
  The old idea that plant proteins must be paired on one plate (rice + beans) is outdated. The body holds an amino acid pool, and it is enough to eat varied sources across the day. Rice and beans is tasty and convenient, but not a biological necessity "in one sitting".
</Callout>

## For muscle

For muscle growth, what matters is not only the amount of protein but leucine — the amino acid that triggers muscle protein synthesis. Animal sources have more of it and a more complete profile, so "per gram" they are slightly more effective. But this is no verdict against a plant-based diet: you can build muscle on it by eating a bit more total protein, keeping variety and, if needed, using concentrates — soy or pea protein are close to complete in their amino acid profile.

## For health and longevity

Here the picture turns toward plant protein. In large observational studies, a higher share of plant protein is linked to lower all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. An important caveat: much of this is a substitution effect — replacing red and processed meat with legumes and nuts removes saturated fat and salt and adds fiber. So what wins is not "plants in themselves" but a healthier diet structure.

## The practical takeaway

Extremes are not needed. A sensible strategy is to shift the balance toward plant protein (legumes, soy, nuts, whole grains) while keeping quality animal sources — fish, eggs, dairy, lean meat — in an amount that suits you. That way you get amino acid completeness, fiber and phytonutrients, and heart benefits all at once.
