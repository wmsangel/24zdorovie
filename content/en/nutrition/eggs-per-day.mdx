In most healthy people, eggs have little effect on blood cholesterol, and about one egg a day is not linked to higher cardiovascular risk in large studies. The old "no more than a couple of eggs a week" rule has been dropped by current guidance: it was built on the logic that "an egg has a lot of cholesterol", which did not hold up in practice. The main caveats are not the eggs themselves but what they are eaten with, plus separate caution in diabetes.

## Where the fear came from and why it is outdated

The logic of the old ban was simple: the yolk has a lot of cholesterol, so it will raise blood cholesterol and harm the heart. The problem is that the body does not work that way. In most people, when cholesterol comes in from food, the liver reduces its own production, and the resulting blood level changes little.

"Bad" LDL cholesterol is affected far more by saturated and trans fats. So current guidance stopped limiting eggs to a hard number and shifted the emphasis to the structure of the diet as a whole.

## How many you can actually eat

Large cohort studies and meta-analyses in healthy people find no link between moderate egg intake (around one a day) and heart attack or stroke. This is a consistent finding across large samples.

## The company matters more than the egg

A common mistake is to blame the egg while forgetting the whole plate. Eggs fried in lots of oil with bacon, sausage and white toast are about saturated fat and salt, not yolk cholesterol. The same egg, boiled or added to vegetables, behaves completely differently.

<Callout type="warning" title="Diabetes is a separate conversation">
  In people with type 2 diabetes the evidence is more conflicting: some studies linked high egg intake to cardiovascular risk. This is not a ban, but a reason not to overdo it and to discuss the amount with a doctor, looking at the whole diet rather than one food.
</Callout>

## What eggs are good for

Set the fears aside and an egg is one of the most nutrient-dense foods: quality protein (about 6 g per egg), choline for brain function, B and D vitamins, and the antioxidants lutein and zeaxanthin for the eyes — at a modest calorie cost. Most of the nutrients are in the yolk, so eating "whites only" for a supposed benefit means losing that benefit.

The bottom line: for a healthy person eggs are not a threat but a convenient source of protein and micronutrients. A benchmark of about one a day is comfortable and undisputed; look not at the egg itself but at what it shares the plate with.
