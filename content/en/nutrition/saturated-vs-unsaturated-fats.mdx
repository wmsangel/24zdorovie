Saturated and unsaturated fats differ by one thing: whether the fatty acid chain contains double bonds. Saturated chains have none, pack together tightly and stay solid at room temperature — butter, cheese fat, coconut oil. Unsaturated chains are kinked by their double bonds, cannot pack, and pour — olive oil, sunflower oil, the fat in nuts and fish. That structural difference translates directly into what the fat does to your LDL cholesterol, and the practical conclusion is narrower than the headlines suggest: what matters is not how much saturated fat you cut, but what you put in its place.

## The difference is one chemical bond

A fatty acid is a chain of carbon atoms with a acid group at one end. Each carbon can hold hydrogen atoms, and the question is whether it is holding as many as it possibly can.

**Saturated** means exactly that: every carbon is saturated with hydrogen, there are no double bonds between carbons, and the chain is straight. Straight chains stack against each other like pencils in a box, which is why a stick of butter holds its shape.

**Monounsaturated** means one double bond. At that bond the chain bends by roughly 30 degrees, and bent chains cannot stack neatly. Olive oil is liquid on the counter for this reason — and thickens in the fridge, because the loose packing still gives way at low temperatures.

**Polyunsaturated** means two or more double bonds and correspondingly more kinks. Sunflower, flaxseed and fish oils stay liquid even when chilled. The same double bonds are also chemically reactive, which is why polyunsaturated oils go rancid faster and should not be stored above the stove or heated hard.

<Callout type="science" title="Why trans fat breaks the rule">
  Almost every natural unsaturated fatty acid has its double bond in the *cis* configuration, with both chain segments on the same side — that is what creates the kink. Industrial hydrogenation flips some of them to *trans*, where the segments sit on opposite sides and the chain runs almost straight again. The result is a molecule that is unsaturated on paper and behaves like a saturated fat in a factory, but is worse than either in the body: it raises LDL and lowers HDL at the same time. This is the reason "unsaturated" alone tells you very little, and why the WHO treats industrial trans fat as a separate category with no safe level.
</Callout>

## What each type does to your cholesterol

This is the mechanism that everything else rests on, and it is not complicated.

Your liver clears LDL particles from the blood using LDL receptors on its surface. Saturated fatty acids — specifically palmitic, myristic and lauric acid — reduce the expression and activity of those receptors. Fewer receptors means slower clearance, and slower clearance means LDL accumulates in the circulation. Unsaturated fatty acids do not suppress the receptors, so replacing one with the other lets clearance recover.

The size of the effect has been quantified precisely, because it can be measured in controlled feeding studies where every meal is provided. The meta-analysis of 60 controlled trials by Mensink and colleagues (2003), whose regression coefficients still underpin the modelling behind current guidelines, produced usable numbers: replacing 1% of daily energy from saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat lowers LDL cholesterol by roughly 0.055 mmol/L (about 2 mg/dL), and with monounsaturated fat by roughly 0.045 mmol/L. Replacing it with carbohydrate lowers LDL less and raises triglycerides while lowering HDL.

Scaled up, a realistic dietary shift of 5% of energy — swapping a daily portion of butter and cheese for olive oil and nuts — moves LDL by something like 0.2–0.3 mmol/L. That is a modest number for one person and a large one across a population.

<Callout type="info" title="The order of the argument matters">
  The chain of evidence runs: saturated fat raises LDL (established in controlled feeding trials, barely disputed) → high LDL causes atherosclerosis (supported by observational data, by genetic studies of people with lifelong high or low LDL, and by what happens when statins lower it) → therefore lowering saturated fat should lower risk. Each link is solid. What is genuinely harder to demonstrate is the whole chain in a single long-term randomised trial, because feeding people a controlled diet for a decade is close to impossible. Most disagreement about saturated fat is really disagreement about how much weight to give that missing final trial.
</Callout>

## The comparison only means something as a swap

Here is where most reporting on this subject goes wrong. "Is saturated fat bad?" has no answer, because a nutrient never leaves a diet without something arriving to replace its calories. The right question is always: instead of what?

The Cochrane review led by Hooper and colleagues in 2020 pooled randomised trials in which people cut saturated fat for at least two years. Overall, cardiovascular events fell by about 17%. But when the reviewers split the trials by what the saturated fat was replaced with, the benefit sat almost entirely in the trials that substituted polyunsaturated vegetable oils. Where carbohydrate or protein took its place, no convincing effect appeared. Total mortality did not shift in any scenario.

Li and colleagues found the same pattern in free-living diets in their 2015 analysis of two large prospective cohorts in the Journal of the American College of Cardiology. Replacing 5% of energy from saturated fat lowered coronary heart disease risk by roughly:

- **25%** when polyunsaturated fat replaced it;
- **15%** when monounsaturated fat replaced it;
- **9%** when whole grains replaced it;
- **nothing measurable** when refined carbohydrate and sugar replaced it.

That last line explains the entire low-fat era. Through the 1980s and 1990s people dutifully cut fat, and the food industry replaced it with sugar and starch — the one substitution the data show does nothing. The diets changed and the outcomes did not, which is exactly what these numbers predict.

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't over-read 'saturated fat was exonerated'">
  Because cutting saturated fat alone does not extend life expectancy in trials, a popular reading concluded that butter is fine in any quantity. That inference does not follow from the data. What the trials show is that the benefit lives in the substitution, not in the subtraction — cutting saturated fat and eating jam instead genuinely achieves nothing, and that is a statement about jam, not a licence on butter.
</Callout>

## Not all saturated fats are the same

The category "saturated fat" bundles together fatty acids that behave differently, and the differences are large enough to matter.

**Stearic acid (C18:0)** is the main saturated fatty acid in cocoa butter and a large share of beef fat. Unlike its shorter relatives, it does not meaningfully raise LDL — the liver converts much of it to oleic acid, a monounsaturated fatty acid. This is one reason dark chocolate does not behave in trials the way its saturated fat content would predict.

**Palmitic (C16:0) and myristic (C14:0) acid** are the LDL-raising members of the family, and they dominate in palm oil, butterfat and fatty meat. Myristic acid has the strongest effect per gram.

**Lauric acid (C12:0)** makes up about half of coconut oil. It raises LDL substantially, and also HDL, which is the origin of the "coconut oil improves your ratio" claim. The total-to-HDL ratio does improve slightly, but LDL — the causal particle — goes up, and the 2017 AHA advisory names coconut oil explicitly among fats to avoid as a primary cooking fat.

There is also the food matrix. The 2020 JACC review by Astrup and colleagues made the case that saturated fat behaves differently depending on the food carrying it: cheese and yoghurt are not associated with the cardiovascular risk their saturated fat content would predict, while processed meat is associated with more risk than its fat alone explains. Calcium, fermentation, protein structure and salt all appear to modify the effect. That review is a live debate rather than settled consensus, and the WHO's 2023 guideline deliberately kept a single nutrient-based limit rather than food-specific ones. The practical takeaway is modest but real: plain yoghurt and cheese are not the same problem as sausage, even at equal saturated fat.

## What to actually do

1. **Make a liquid vegetable oil your default fat.** Olive oil for dressings and everyday cooking, refined olive or canola for higher heat. This single change does more than any amount of label reading.
2. **Keep saturated fat bounded, not banished.** Under 10% of energy — about 22 g a day on 2,000 kcal, or 15 g if your LDL is elevated. Cheese, butter and fatty meat all fit; all three on the same day generally do not.
3. **Watch what fills the gap.** Removing butter and adding jam is not progress. Nuts, olive oil, oily fish, avocado and legumes are the replacements the trials rewarded.
4. **Treat coconut oil as a flavour, not a health food.** It is the most saturated fat in a normal kitchen.
5. **Read the ingredient list for "partially hydrogenated".** Trans fat is a separate and worse category, and it hides in pastry, coatings and cheap frying fat rather than announcing itself on the nutrition panel.
6. **Eat oily fish twice a week.** Mackerel, herring and sardines are cheap and deliver the omega-3 fatty acids that no vegetable oil provides in usable form.

Reduced to a sentence: the fat you remove matters far less than the fat you put in its place. Once you stop asking which foods are forbidden and start asking what replaces them, the apparent contradictions in this field mostly disappear.
