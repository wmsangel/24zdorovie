Adults need roughly 25 g of fiber a day for women and 38 g for men, or about 14 g per 1,000 kcal eaten. Most people in Western countries get 15 to 18 g, which means the typical shortfall is around 10 g a day. Closing that gap is one of the few dietary changes with consistent, large-scale evidence behind it: in a Lancet analysis of 185 prospective studies and 58 trials, people eating 25–29 g a day had 15 to 30 percent lower rates of all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and colorectal cancer than those eating the least.

## What fiber actually is

Fiber is the part of plant food that human digestive enzymes cannot break down. It reaches the large intestine largely intact, which is precisely where its usefulness begins.

There, gut bacteria ferment much of it and produce short-chain fatty acids — mostly butyrate, propionate, and acetate. Butyrate is the preferred fuel of the cells lining the colon. Propionate reaches the liver and influences cholesterol synthesis. This fermentation is the mechanistic reason fiber shows up in outcomes as varied as blood lipids, glucose control, and colorectal cancer risk.

### Two types, two jobs

**Soluble fiber** dissolves in water and forms a viscous gel. It slows gastric emptying, which flattens post-meal glucose peaks, and it binds bile acids in the gut, forcing the liver to pull cholesterol from circulation to make more. Sources: oats, barley, legumes, apples, citrus, psyllium.

**Insoluble fiber** does not dissolve. It adds bulk and holds water, shortening transit time through the colon. Sources: wheat bran, whole grains, vegetable skins, nuts, seeds.

Almost every whole plant food contains both, in different proportions. This is the strongest argument for getting fiber from food rather than a tub of powder.

<Callout type="science" title="The dose-response is unusually linear">
  In the 2019 Lancet series, benefit continued to rise across the whole observed range up to about 35 g a day, with no clear plateau. That is uncommon in nutrition, where most curves flatten early. The practical reading: any increase toward the target is worth making, and there is no threshold you must hit before benefit begins.
</Callout>

The simplest rule of thumb, if you would rather not memorise a table, is 14 g per 1,000 kcal. It scales automatically with body size and activity, which is why it is the form the reference intakes are actually derived in.

Two observations fall out of this table. First, legumes are in a category of their own: a single cup of beans or lentils delivers more than half a day's target, which no vegetable or fruit comes close to. Second, the gap between refined and whole versions of the same food is larger than people expect — white bread carries about 0.8 g of fiber per slice against roughly 2 g for whole wheat.

### Practical ways to add 10 g

- Half a cup of beans added to a soup, salad, or stew: **+7 g**
- Swapping white rice for barley or bulgur at one meal: **+4 g**
- A cup of berries instead of a banana as a snack: **+5 g**
- Two tablespoons of chia in yoghurt: **+10 g**
- Leaving the skin on potatoes and apples: **+2 g**

You do not need all five. Any two of them close the average shortfall.

## What fiber does and does not do

### Well-supported effects

**Cardiovascular risk.** Each additional 7 g a day is associated with roughly 9 percent lower coronary heart disease risk in pooled cohort data. Soluble fiber directly lowers LDL cholesterol: about 3 g of oat beta-glucan a day produces a modest but reliable 5 to 10 mg/dL reduction.

**Blood glucose.** Viscous fiber slows carbohydrate absorption, reducing post-meal glucose excursions. In people with type 2 diabetes, higher-fiber diets improve HbA1c by around 0.3 to 0.5 percentage points.

**Bowel function.** Insoluble fiber plus adequate fluid increases stool weight and frequency. Psyllium in particular has good trial evidence for chronic constipation.

**Colorectal cancer.** A dose-response meta-analysis found about a 10 percent lower risk per 10 g of daily fiber, with whole grains showing the strongest signal.

**Satiety and body weight.** Fiber adds volume without calories and slows gastric emptying. The effect on weight is real but modest — typically 1 to 2 kg over a year in trials, not a transformation.

### Less certain

Fiber's effect on diverticular disease is weaker than the textbooks once claimed, and the old advice to avoid nuts and seeds has been abandoned. Claims about fiber "detoxifying" the body have no mechanism worth discussing.

<Callout type="info" title="Fiber and the microbiome">
  Different fibers feed different bacterial populations. Inulin and fructans from onions, garlic, leeks, and chicory; resistant starch from cooled potatoes, rice, and green bananas; beta-glucans from oats and barley. Variety of plant sources appears to matter more for microbial diversity than total grams alone — one reason to rotate foods rather than eat the same bowl of oats every morning.
</Callout>

## Raising intake without discomfort

The bloating people report when they "go healthy" is real and predictable. Gut bacteria ferment fiber and produce gas; a sudden doubling of substrate outpaces the microbiome's capacity to adapt.

The fix is straightforward:

1. **Increase by about 5 g per week**, not all at once. Reaching the target over four to six weeks is a reasonable pace.
2. **Drink more fluid.** Fiber that draws water into the gut without enough water available makes constipation worse, not better. Roughly 30 ml per kg of body weight a day is a sensible baseline.
3. **Distribute it.** Three moderate fiber servings are tolerated far better than one enormous one.
4. **Cook legumes properly.** Soaking dried beans and discarding the water removes some of the oligosaccharides responsible for gas. Canned and rinsed beans are also gentler than home-cooked from dry.

Most people find that transient bloating settles within two to three weeks as the bacterial population shifts.

<Callout type="warning" title="When to slow down or ask a clinician">
  If you have inflammatory bowel disease, a known bowel stricture, gastroparesis, or have recently had abdominal surgery, do not raise fiber intake without medical advice. In irritable bowel syndrome, high-FODMAP fiber sources such as beans, onions, and wheat can worsen symptoms, while soluble low-FODMAP options like psyllium and oats are usually tolerated. Persistent bloating, blood in the stool, or unintended weight loss are reasons to be examined rather than to adjust your diet.
</Callout>

## Should you take a supplement?

Isolated fibers have a legitimate but narrow role. Psyllium husk has the best evidence: it improves both constipation and, at 7 to 10 g a day, LDL cholesterol. Inulin and partially hydrolysed guar gum have some support. Methylcellulose and calcium polycarbophil work for bulk but do not ferment, so they lack the metabolic effects.

What no supplement provides is everything else that arrives with fiber-rich food: potassium, magnesium, folate, polyphenols, and a mixture of fiber types with different fermentation profiles. In cohort studies, the mortality associations attach to fiber from food, not from supplements.

The reasonable position is to build the diet first and use a supplement to cover a stubborn residual gap.

## A day that hits the target

Roughly 35 g without effort or unusual ingredients:

- **Breakfast.** Oats (50 g dry) with raspberries and a tablespoon of chia — 12 g
- **Lunch.** Lentil soup with two slices of whole grain bread — 12 g
- **Snack.** An apple with skin and a handful of almonds — 7 g
- **Dinner.** Roasted vegetables with barley and fish — 6 g

Note how little of this is exotic. Fiber gaps are rarely a knowledge problem; they are a legume problem. Most people who add beans or lentils to two meals a week and switch one refined grain for a whole one land within range.
