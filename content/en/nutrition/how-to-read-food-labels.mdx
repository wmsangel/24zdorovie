The single most useful thing on a food package is the ingredient list, because it is the one part that cannot misrepresent proportions: ingredients must be listed in descending order of weight. The front of the box is advertising, the Nutrition Facts panel is arithmetic, and the ingredient list is the closest thing to a plain answer about what you are buying. Turn the package over, read the first three or four entries, and only then look at the numbers.

## Why the ingredient list outranks everything else

Descending order of weight is not an industry courtesy. It is written into Codex Alimentarius, the FAO/WHO standards framework that national rules are built on: all ingredients shall be listed in descending order of ingoing weight at the time of manufacture. US regulation says the same thing, and so does Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, the European law that has governed food information to consumers since December 2014.

That single requirement does a lot of work. A "made with whole grain" cracker that lists refined flour first and whole grain flour fourth, after sugar and oil, contains modest amounts of whole grain no matter how much wheat is drawn on the box. A "real fruit" bar that lists fruit after two sweeteners is a candy bar with fruit in it.

There is a second, subtler read. Notice where recognizable food ends and formulation begins. A long ingredient list is not automatically bad — leaveners, cultures, and preservatives are often sensible. But if the list runs out of things you could buy separately by the fourth entry and continues for two more lines of flavors, gums, and colors, you are holding an assembly rather than a food.

<Callout type="tip" title="One habit is enough">
  Do not try to master the whole panel. Build one reflex: before an unfamiliar product goes in the cart, flip it and read the first three ingredients. It takes five seconds and filters out most bad purchases. Percentages, conversions, and the rest are for fine-tuning, and you will rarely need them in the aisle.
</Callout>

## The techniques worth recognizing

### Splitting the sugars

This is the most common trick and the most elegant one. Manufacturers know that shoppers glance at the top of the ingredient list and that sugar in first position looks bad. The fix is not to use less sugar but to use several kinds.

Twenty grams split into corn syrup, dextrose, maltodextrin, apple juice concentrate, and molasses at four grams apiece becomes five separate entries, each of which comfortably lands below the flour and the oil. Nothing has been falsified: every weight is accurate and the order is correct. The product is exactly as sweet as before.

The vocabulary is longer than most people expect: sucrose, glucose, fructose, dextrose, maltose, corn syrup, high-fructose corn syrup, invert syrup, cane juice, evaporated cane syrup, molasses, malt extract, maltodextrin, brown rice syrup, honey, agave nectar, coconut sugar, fruit juice concentrate, fruit puree concentrate. Juice concentrates deserve special mention, because they let a package carry "no sugar added" while adding sugar.

The US label defuses this directly. Since the 2016 overhaul, Nutrition Facts panels carry a mandatory Added Sugars line with its own %DV, so the total is printed for you regardless of how many names it was split into. On European-style labels there is no such line — only "of which sugars," which combines added and naturally occurring sugar — and you have to reason about it yourself.

### Serving size, set by the seller

The US panel reports everything per serving, and serving sizes are supposed to reflect what people actually eat in one sitting. In practice there is still room to maneuver, and the number that protects you is the one printed directly above it: servings per container.

A 20-ounce bottle marked as 2.5 servings, a bag of chips at three servings, a pint of ice cream at three or four, a jar of sauce at eight — in each case the headline calorie figure applies to a fraction of what most people consume at once. Multiplying is the entire skill. If the panel says 140 calories and 9 g of added sugar, and the container holds 2.5 servings, the bottle you are about to drink holds 350 calories and 22 g of added sugar.

European labels sidestep this by requiring values per 100 g or 100 mL, which makes products directly comparable but tells you nothing about the portion you will actually eat. Neither format is complete; both require one arithmetic step.

<Callout type="warning" title="Drinks are where this bites hardest">
  Nobody reseals a bottle after 8 ounces. Beverages, ice cream, and family-size snack bags are exactly the products where the labeled serving is least likely to match behavior, and they are also the products where sugar and calories are densest. When you compare two drinks, compare whole containers, not servings.
</Callout>

### Words with no legal content

Front-of-package language falls into three tiers. Some claims are defined with hard numeric thresholds and cannot be used freely. Some are defined loosely or only in certain jurisdictions. And some are pure atmosphere.

Claims about absent ingredients deserve their own note. "Cholesterol-free" sunflower oil is accurate, because no vegetable oil contains cholesterol. "Non-GMO" salt is accurate for the same reason: there is no such thing as GM salt. These claims are not lies, they are implications — the suggestion that competitors have a problem this brand has solved. Once you notice the pattern it is hard to unsee.

## The lines that actually matter

Most of the panel is background. Six things carry the signal.

**The ingredient list.** Everything above applies here first.

**Added sugars.** On a US label this is a separate mandatory line with a %DV based on a 50 g daily reference. On a European label you get only total sugars and must subtract what is naturally there — plain yogurt and milk carry roughly 4.7 g of lactose per 100 g, so a yogurt declaring 12 g has about 7 g added.

**Saturated fat.** Its own line under total fat. Total fat by itself says little: olive oil and almonds are mostly fat and are not the problem.

<ReadAlso href="/en/nutrition/fiber-how-much-you-need" title="Fiber: How Much You Need and Where to Get It" />

**Sodium.** US labels declare sodium in milligrams; most of the rest of the world declares salt in grams, and the two are not interchangeable — sodium is about 40% of salt by weight. Multiply sodium by roughly 2.5 to get salt, or divide salt by 2.5 to get sodium. WHO recommends adults consume less than 2,000 mg of sodium per day, equivalent to less than 5 g of salt, while global average intake in 2021 was 4,278 mg per day — more than twice the recommendation. Packaged foods, not the shaker, supply most of it in high-income countries.

**Fiber and protein.** The two lines you want to be large. For bread, cereal, and crackers, look for products where fiber is a meaningful fraction rather than a rounding error, and treat protein as a proxy for how filling the food will be.

<Callout type="science" title="The 5 and 20 rule">
  The percent Daily Value column exists to save you from memorizing reference amounts, and FDA gives one rule for reading it: 5% DV or less per serving is low, and 20% DV or more is high. Point it at saturated fat, sodium, and added sugars and you want small numbers. Point it at fiber, potassium, calcium, vitamin D, and iron and you want large ones. Note that %DV is calculated per serving, not per package, so it inherits every serving-size caveat above. The reference diet behind the column is 2,000 calories a day — a labeling convention, not a personal recommendation.
</Callout>

## Fifteen seconds at the shelf

1. **Turn the package around.** The front is marketing; the back is regulated.
2. **Read the first three ingredients.** Sugar or one of its aliases in the top three, in something that is not dessert, is enough to put the box back.
3. **Count the sugar names.** Three or more distinct sweeteners in one list means splitting, whatever the total looks like.
4. **Check servings per container.** Then multiply the panel by the number of servings you will realistically eat.
5. **Scan three %DVs:** added sugars, sodium, saturated fat. Twenty percent or more per serving is high.
6. **Look for something on the plus side:** fiber at 10% DV or better, and a protein figure worth the calories.

The point of the sequence is that it is short enough to survive contact with a real grocery store. A full label analysis takes minutes, which is why almost nobody does it twice.

<Callout type="info" title="Audit five regular purchases">
  One-off products barely move your diet; the things in your cart every week move it a lot. Sit down once at home with five staples — bread, yogurt, cereal or granola, a favorite sauce, and a frozen or prepared meal — and read them properly. This is where unnoticed sugar and sodium usually live, and swapping two or three of those items does more than any general resolution to eat better.
</Callout>

## What labels cannot tell you

The method has limits, and knowing them keeps you from overtrusting it.

A label says nothing about your portion. An excellent product eaten in triple quantity performs worse than an average one eaten sensibly, and no panel will warn you about that.

A label does not directly report how processed a food is. The ingredient list gives indirect evidence — length, the presence of things you could buy on their own — but there is no line for it.

And a label does not sort foods into good and bad. It lets you compare like with like: two breads, two yogurts, two pasta sauces. Comparing the panel of bread to the panel of olive oil produces nothing useful.

Simplified front-of-pack systems — traffic lights, summary scores, high-in warning symbols — are worth a mention here. They speed up choices and are genuinely useful as a first filter, but each compresses several nutrients into one symbol, and compression loses detail: a well-scoring product may lean on sweeteners, and a poorly scoring one may be nuts or olive oil, categories these scales handle badly. Use them to narrow the shelf, then read the back of the two or three packages that survive.

None of this is about finding a perfect product. It is about not paying for the box. Two granolas sitting next to each other routinely differ by 12 g of sugar per 100 g, and the only place that difference is visible is the side where the wording is set by regulators rather than by a marketing team.
