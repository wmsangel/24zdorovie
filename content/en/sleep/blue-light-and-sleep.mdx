Evening light suppresses melatonin and shifts the body clock, and the blue spectrum affects this most — that part is true. But the popular conclusion, "screens destroy sleep, filter glasses save it", is a big oversimplification. In practice a phone is dimmer than the daytime sky, its effect is moderate, and blue-filter glasses barely help in studies. What matters far more is overall brightness, timing, and what exactly you are doing on that screen into the small hours.

## How light affects the clock

The retina has special light-sensitive cells, especially sensitive to the blue spectrum. Through them, light tells the brain "it is daytime" and slows the production of melatonin — the hormone that prepares the body for sleep. So bright light late in the evening shifts the body clock later and makes it harder to fall asleep.

This is a real mechanism, and the screen fears are built on it. In a well-known study, reading on a tablet before bed suppressed melatonin and shifted the rhythm compared with a paper book. But this effect has a scale, and it is often exaggerated.

<Callout type="science" title="A question of scale">
  A smartphone or tablet emits light at the level of tens of lux; the daytime sky, tens of thousands. A screen does affect melatonin, but the effect is modest next to daylight. So "blue light from the phone" is real, but usually not the main cause of poor sleep.
</Callout>

## What actually matters more

Often the root of the problem is not the light spectrum but that the screen **keeps you awake**: an engaging video, a feed, work email and alarming news stimulate the brain more than any filter calms it. Plus the simple fact that "five more minutes" turns into an hour past midnight.

So the effective levers are about behaviour and schedule:

- in the evening, dim the room's overall light, not just the screen;
- during the day, on the contrary, catch bright light — it stabilises the clock more than any glasses;
- keep a consistent bedtime;
- do not bring work and anxiety-inducing tasks into bed.

## The practical takeaway

There is no need to declare war on screens and buy glasses. It is enough to dim the brightness 30–60 minutes before bed, drop stimulating activities and not stay up into the small hours — and get more light during the day. It is duller than "magic glasses", but it is what works.
