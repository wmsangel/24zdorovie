For a standard cup of coffee — roughly 100 mg of caffeine — the safe cut-off is about 9 hours before bed; for a large dose of 200 mg or more it stretches to 13 hours, according to a 2023 meta-analysis of controlled trials. With an 11 pm bedtime that means your last ordinary coffee around 2 pm. But the number matters less than the mechanism behind it: caffeine does not supply energy, it blocks your perception of the tiredness you have already accumulated, which is why "I drink coffee late and sleep great" almost never means your sleep was untouched.

## What caffeine actually does

Every hour you are awake, adenosine accumulates in the brain as a by-product of neuronal energy metabolism. It binds to A1 and A2A receptors and dampens neural activity, which you experience as gradually mounting sleepiness. Sleep researchers call this homeostatic sleep pressure — the longer you have been awake, the heavier it gets. Sleep clears it, and the next morning the cycle restarts from near zero.

Caffeine resembles adenosine closely enough to occupy the same receptors, but not closely enough to activate them. It sits in the lock without turning it. The adenosine itself does not disappear; it keeps building up in the background while the brain stops receiving the signal.

Three practical consequences follow, and they are rarely spelled out.

**Caffeine is not a source of energy.** A cup of coffee contains no calories and nothing that replenishes any resource. It releases a brake rather than adding fuel. The alertness you feel afterwards is your own baseline activity, temporarily un-inhibited.

**Your sleep debt keeps growing.** Nothing about caffeine substitutes for what sleep does — memory consolidation, metabolic and immune housekeeping, clearance of metabolic waste. Four hours of sleep plus three coffees is still four hours of sleep; you have only made yourself worse at noticing it.

**The crash is built in.** When caffeine clears the receptors, all the adenosine that accumulated in the meantime binds at once. That is the abrupt slump a few hours after a large dose, usually misattributed to a "sugar drop".

<Callout type="science" title="Why coffee is a poor substitute for a night of sleep">
  Caffeine restores simple vigilance reasonably well — staying awake, reacting to signals on time. It performs far worse on complex functions such as working memory, risk assessment and cognitive flexibility. The real hazard is that self-assessment recovers along with vigilance: a sleep-deprived person on caffeine feels capable while the quality of their decisions remains impaired.
</Callout>

## Half-life: why "it wears off in five hours" is misleading

In healthy adults, caffeine's half-life averages 4 to 6 hours — the time it takes to eliminate half a dose. So of 200 mg taken at 3 pm, roughly 100 mg is still circulating at 8 pm and around 50 mg at 1 am. That residue is the equivalent of drinking half an espresso as you get into bed.

The word carrying all the weight in that sentence is "averages". Between healthy individuals the range runs from about 2 to 10 hours, and it is a stable personal trait, not measurement noise. The same after-dinner cappuccino is gone by midnight for one person and still working at sunrise for another.

### What sets your clearance rate

Around 95% of caffeine is broken down in the liver by the enzyme CYP1A2. Anything that changes that enzyme's activity changes how long caffeine stays with you.

**Genetics.** Carriers of the fast CYP1A2 variant metabolise caffeine substantially quicker than slow metabolisers. This explains a lot of apparent willpower differences: people who stop at one morning cup often do so not out of discipline but because a second one visibly wrecks their night.

**Hormonal contraceptives.** Oestrogen-containing formulations inhibit CYP1A2 and roughly double caffeine's half-life. In practice, if you are on a combined oral contraceptive, your personal cut-off belongs several hours earlier than the standard advice.

**Pregnancy.** The slowdown is larger still and deepens through gestation, with half-life in the third trimester reported at two to three times baseline. This is one reason pregnancy has its own, stricter limit rather than a proportional adjustment.

**Smoking.** Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons in tobacco smoke induce CYP1A2 and nearly halve caffeine's half-life. Hence an underappreciated side effect of quitting: your habitual five cups start behaving like eight, producing anxiety, jitteriness and insomnia that get blamed on nicotine withdrawal. Cutting coffee by about a third at the same time as quitting prevents most of it.

**Age and liver function.** Clearance slows in older adults and in liver impairment. Children and adolescents metabolise caffeine faster than adults but are more sensitive to its central effects.

<Callout type="info" title="Finding your own type without a genetic test">
  CYP1A2 tests are sold direct to consumers, but you do not need one. Run a home experiment instead: take 200 mg of caffeine (two mugs of filter coffee) exactly 6 hours before your usual bedtime, three nights running, changing nothing else. If sleep onset drifts by more than 20 to 30 minutes, or the night becomes noticeably more broken, treat yourself as a slow metaboliser and set your cut-off at 11–12 hours rather than 9.
</Callout>

## The answer to the question in the title

The 2023 meta-analysis by Gardiner and colleagues pooled experimental studies in which caffeine was given in controlled doses and sleep was measured objectively — by polysomnography and actigraphy, not questionnaires. From the pooled data the authors derived quantitative guidance on when to stop so that caffeine does not shorten total sleep time:

- **a standard cup of coffee, ~100 mg** — about 8.8 hours before bed;
- **a large dose, ~200 mg and above** — up to 13 hours before bed.

An earlier trial by Drake and colleagues in 2013 shows why folk guidance falls short. Participants took 400 mg of caffeine 0, 3 and 6 hours before bed. Sleep disruption was significant in all three conditions, including the dose taken six hours ahead — and critically, participants in that condition did not notice any impairment, while objectively losing around an hour of sleep.

The practical rule that follows: **go by the clock and the dose, not by how you feel**.

## "But I fall asleep fine" is not evidence

The standard objection to any cut-off is that the person drinks an espresso after dinner and is asleep in ten minutes. It holds up right until someone measures the sleep rather than asking about it.

The systematic review by Clark and Landolt summarises the pattern: caffeine lengthens sleep onset latency, shortens total sleep time and — most importantly — reduces slow-wave sleep and delta power, the objective marker of sleep depth. It simultaneously increases the share of light stage 1 sleep and the number of awakenings.

The key point is that none of this requires insomnia to be present. You can fall asleep quickly, log a full eight hours and still get a night that is measurably shallower than it should have been. Subjectively it registers as "slept fine, woke up flat" — which is precisely the state that produces one more cup in the morning.

There is a circadian effect layered on top. Evening caffeine delays the rise of melatonin and shifts the internal clock later. So it is not only that tonight is worse; tomorrow night is harder to start on time.

## How much caffeine is in what you drink

The most common counting error is thinking in cups rather than milligrams. An espresso in a small cup carries less caffeine than a large mug of filter coffee, even though it tastes far stronger. Variation within a single category is wide too, driven by bean variety, grind, brew method and serving size.

Hidden sources deserve their own line: combination painkillers containing caffeine, cold and flu remedies, fat burners, chewing gums and sports gels. Someone convinced they drink "only two coffees" can easily be at 500 mg once a pre-workout scoop and a headache tablet are counted.

## Safe intake: the EFSA numbers

The 2015 scientific opinion from the European Food Safety Authority remains the standard reference:

- **up to 400 mg per day** from all sources raises no safety concern for healthy adults;
- **up to 200 mg in a single dose**, including within two hours of intense exercise;
- **up to 200 mg per day** during pregnancy and breastfeeding;
- **about 3 mg per kg of body weight per day** as the reference point for children and adolescents.

One caveat matters more than the numbers themselves: 400 mg is a general safety ceiling, not a sleep threshold. You can stay comfortably inside the daily allowance and still ruin the night by taking all of it at 6 pm. Dose and timing are independent variables and both need watching.

<Callout type="warning" title="Who needs tighter limits than the standard advice">
  **Pregnancy and breastfeeding** — no more than 200 mg a day; clearance is markedly slower and caffeine crosses the placenta. **Arrhythmias, uncontrolled hypertension or ischaemic heart disease** — discuss intake with your cardiologist; caffeine can provoke ectopic beats and tachycardia in susceptible people. **Anxiety and panic disorder** — caffeine reliably amplifies symptoms and can trigger panic attacks in predisposed individuals, and substantial reduction is standard clinical advice. **Energy drinks** warrant separate caution: a high dose delivered quickly alongside sugar and other stimulants is a poor fit for adolescents, and mixing them with alcohol is specifically discouraged — caffeine masks the sensation of intoxication without correcting impaired coordination or judgement. If you take regular medication, check for interactions in both directions: caffeine affects the metabolism of some drugs, and several drugs affect its own.
</Callout>

## Tolerance and withdrawal

Tolerance to some of caffeine's effects develops within days of regular use: the brain upregulates adenosine receptors to compensate for the blockade. This is why the habitual cup eventually stops producing a lift and merely restores baseline — you drink coffee to stop feeling bad rather than to feel good.

The important nuance is that tolerance to the subjective buzz builds faster than tolerance to the sleep disruption. People stop noticing the effect of an evening coffee long before that coffee stops damaging their sleep. That mismatch is the entire basis of the "it doesn't affect me" illusion.

Caffeine withdrawal is a well-characterised syndrome, not folklore. After stopping regular intake, symptoms appear within 12 to 24 hours, peak on day one or two, and resolve over roughly 2 to 9 days. The typical picture is headache (the most common symptom), fatigue, poor concentration, irritability, and sometimes nausea and muscle aches. It occurs at habitual intakes as low as about 100 mg a day — a single cup.

## Energy drinks and teenagers

Two facts combine badly in adolescence. First, puberty shifts circadian timing later as a matter of biology, so natural sleep onset drifts while school start times do not — chronic sleep restriction arrives without any help from caffeine. Second, the 3 mg per kg reference means a 50 kg teenager reaches their daily ceiling at 150 mg, which one large energy drink exceeds on its own.

Temple's 2019 review of caffeine in children and adolescents describes the loop clearly: energy drinks are consumed to compensate for insufficient sleep, they degrade the following night, and that increases demand for the next can. Layered on top are a consistent association between energy drink use and other risk-taking behaviours, and the delivery pattern itself — a can is drunk in minutes, whereas coffee is sipped over an hour.

Practical family guidance: no energy drinks before 18, no caffeine after lunch on school days, and an audit of the non-obvious sources — caffeine tablets, gum, and pre-workout supplements among teenagers who train.

## A plan for moving your cut-off

1. **Spend a week counting milligrams, not cups.** Log coffee, tea, cola, chocolate, painkillers and supplements. The total usually surprises people.
2. **Set your cut-off from the table.** Count back 9 hours from your actual bedtime. If you take hormonal contraceptives, are pregnant, or suspect slow metabolism, use 11 to 12 hours.
3. **Move it gradually.** Shifting by an hour every three or four days passes unnoticed; dropping the evening cup outright often produces a two-day headache.
4. **Cut the total by 25% at a time** if you are above 400 mg. The easy method is blending regular and decaf in a changing ratio.
5. **Move volume to the morning rather than removing it.** Your daily total can stay the same: a cup at 8 am and another at noon affects sleep incomparably less than the same two cups at noon and 5 pm.
6. **Judge the result after two weeks by how mornings feel**, not by how fast you fall asleep. The sense of waking up restored is usually the first thing to return.
7. **Stop propping up sleep debt with caffeine.** If your alertness exists only on top of a dose, the problem is the sleep, not the coffee.

Caffeine is among the best-studied and broadly safest psychoactive substances available, and giving it up for the sake of sleep is not the goal. The goal is to stop treating it as energy and start treating it as a drug with known pharmacokinetics: it has a dose, a duration and a last acceptable time of day. Getting the third one right usually does more for your sleep than the entire wind-down routine that follows it.
