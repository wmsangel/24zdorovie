Good sleep hygiene is not a checklist of thirty rituals. It comes down to five or six habits with real physiology behind them: a fixed wake time, a cool dark bedroom, bright light in the morning, no caffeine after midday, and no alcohol as a nightcap. Almost everything else being sold as sleep optimisation — blue-light glasses, magnesium drinks, elaborate wind-down routines — rests on much thinner evidence than its marketing suggests. And there is one boundary worth knowing before you start: once insomnia is chronic, sleep hygiene on its own does not treat it, and cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia is the recommended first-line care.

## What sleep hygiene can and cannot do

The phrase dates to the 1970s, when it described a one-page handout a clinician could give a patient. Since then it has accumulated dozens of rules of unclear origin and become a marketing umbrella for mattresses, lamps and gummies.

Two situations need to be separated.

**You sleep reasonably well and want to sleep better.** Sleep hygiene is exactly the right tool. Most poor sleep in otherwise healthy adults has mundane causes: an afternoon coffee, an overheated bedroom, a schedule that drifts by two hours at the weekend, a phone in bed. Remove those and the improvement is quick and obvious.

**You have chronic insomnia.** Here sleep hygiene as a standalone intervention does not work. The 2021 American Academy of Sleep Medicine clinical practice guideline explicitly recommends against sleep hygiene as a single-component therapy for chronic insomnia, because trials consistently show it underperforms compared with multicomponent CBT-I. This is not a technicality — it is the most common reason people spend a year tightening their routine and get nowhere.

<Callout type="info" title="How much sleep is enough">
  The AASM and Sleep Research Society consensus is that adults should regularly sleep 7 hours or more per night. There is no hard upper limit, though routinely needing more than 9 hours in adulthood is worth investigating — it more often signals a problem than good health. Note that 7–9 hours means time asleep, not time in bed; budget an extra 20 to 30 minutes for falling asleep.
</Callout>

## The rules with strong evidence

### 1. The same wake time, seven days a week

If you only change one thing, change this. The circadian system is entrained mainly by morning light and by the moment you get up, not by when you turn the lights off. Sleeping in by two or three hours on Saturday is physiologically equivalent to flying two time zones west and back — the "social jetlag" that makes Monday morning feel like a hangover.

Fixing bedtime, by contrast, is unhelpful and sometimes counterproductive: lying down on schedule when you are not sleepy is practice at being awake in bed. Hold the wake time, let bedtime float, and sleepiness will migrate into place within a week or two.

### 2. Keep the bedroom cool: 17–19 °C

Falling asleep depends on core body temperature dropping by roughly 0.5–1 °C. A warm room blocks that directly — the body cannot offload heat, so the transition stalls. Around 17–19 °C (63–66 °F) suits most adults, but the precise number matters less than the bedroom being distinctly cooler than the rest of the home.

A warm shower 60 to 90 minutes before bed helps for the same reason, counterintuitive as it sounds: it dilates skin blood vessels, so the body sheds heat faster afterwards and core temperature falls more steeply.

### 3. Light: plenty in the morning, little at night

Light is by far the strongest external signal to the body clock. The practical minimum: 10 to 20 minutes of outdoor light within an hour of waking — even an overcast day is an order of magnitude brighter than indoor lighting — and dim, warm light for the 90 minutes before bed.

At night the room should be genuinely dark. Street lamps, standby LEDs and a hallway light all fragment sleep whether or not you consciously wake. Blackout curtains or a sleep mask cost almost nothing and outperform most "sleep tech".

### 4. Caffeine: draw the line at midday

Caffeine's half-life in adults averages about 5 hours, but the spread is enormous — anywhere from 2 to 10 hours depending on genetics, smoking, oral contraceptives and liver function.

A 2023 meta-analysis of experimental studies produced usable numbers: to avoid measurable loss of sleep, a standard coffee (around 100 mg of caffeine) should be finished roughly 8.8 hours before bed, and a large dose (200 mg or more) as much as 13 hours before. For an 11 pm bedtime, that puts the last coffee at about 2 pm.

The point is not only about falling asleep. Even when you drop off easily after an evening coffee, the proportion of deep slow-wave sleep drops — and that is where the next morning's flatness comes from.

### 5. Alcohol is not a sleep aid

Alcohol shortens the time it takes to fall asleep, and that is where the benefit ends. As it is metabolised it produces rebound arousal in the second half of the night: it suppresses REM sleep early, causes a REM rebound later, fragments sleep, worsens snoring and obstructive apnoea, and adds awakenings through its diuretic effect.

A workable rule is to finish the last drink at least three to four hours before bed, and to keep the dose small. "A glass of wine to help me sleep" is the single most common reason someone spends eight hours in bed and still wakes up unrefreshed.

### 6. The bed is for sleep only

Known clinically as stimulus control, this rule has evidence strong enough that it works as a standalone therapy. The goal is to rebuild the conditioned association between bed and sleep after it has been eroded by working, scrolling and worrying in the same place.

Concretely: go to bed only when sleepy; if you are still awake after about 20 minutes, get up, leave the room, do something calm in low light, and return when sleepiness comes back. Do not check the clock — a bedside clock turns waiting for sleep into a measurable failure, which is precisely the arousal you are trying to avoid.

### 7. Regular physical activity

Regular aerobic and resistance training produce a modest but reliable improvement in sleep quality and a shorter time to fall asleep across many randomised trials. The old warning against evening exercise is overstated: hard training in the hour before bed does interfere for some people, but a session two to three hours before bed is neutral or beneficial for most.

### 8. Dinner timing and fluids

A heavy meal immediately before bed delays sleep onset and provokes reflux once you lie down. The opposite extreme is no better: trying to sleep hungry produces awakenings. The sensible middle is a main meal two to three hours before bed, with a light snack later if needed.

Taper fluids in the last 60 to 90 minutes if you routinely get up to use the bathroom at night.

## The rules that are weaker than their reputation

Not everything on the standard sleep hygiene handout survived testing. As the 2015 review of the empirical evidence noted, several long-standing recommendations were extrapolated from laboratory findings rather than tested as interventions in ordinary sleepers.

### 9. Blue-light filters

The idea is elegant: blue wavelengths suppress melatonin most strongly, so filtering them should protect sleep. In practice the effect has been hard to demonstrate.

A 2023 Cochrane review of blue-light filtering spectacle lenses found no clear evidence of benefit for sleep quality. Studies of phone night modes report small and inconsistent effects, and screen brightness — plus what is actually on the screen, whether that is social media, work email or three more episodes — moves sleep onset far more than colour temperature does.

The conclusion is not "turn night mode off", it is "do not rely on it". Cutting screen time and dimming the room work better than shifting the spectrum.

### 10. Magnesium and bedtime "sleep drinks"

Magnesium has become a folk hypnotic on very little data. A 2021 systematic review of oral magnesium for insomnia in older adults located only a few small, low-quality trials with inconsistent effects on sleep onset, and there is no large, well-conducted trial in the general adult population.

That does not make magnesium useless. Correcting a documented deficiency is worthwhile and typical doses are safe. But using it as a substitute for a sleeping pill means paying for an effect that may be entirely explained by expectation and by the calming ritual around it.

The same caveat applies to blends of glycine, L-theanine, chamomile and valerian: individual small studies exist, guideline-grade effects do not.

### 11. Melatonin as a sleeping pill

Melatonin is a timing signal, not a sedative. It performs well where the clock is misaligned: jet lag, shift work, and delayed sleep phase in extreme night owls. In those cases a small dose (0.5–1 mg) taken several hours before the target bedtime is what the physiology calls for.

For ordinary insomnia in adults, the effect is small — it shortens sleep onset by a matter of minutes on average. The 5 and 10 mg doses that dominate retail shelves offer no advantage and more often produce morning grogginess.

### 12. Sleep trackers

Rings and watches are useful for one thing: spotting patterns over months. How much your schedule drifts, what alcohol does to your night, how much time you actually spend in bed. Consumer devices classify sleep stages with only moderate accuracy, so "42 minutes of deep sleep" should not be read literally.

There is also a documented downside — orthosomnia, an anxious preoccupation with tracker scores that itself degrades sleep. If the morning score sets the tone of your day, the device is costing you more than it gives.

<Callout type="science" title="Why sleep hygiene fails against insomnia">
  Chronic insomnia is sustained by hyperarousal rather than by habits: worry about sleep raises arousal, arousal prevents sleep, the failure deepens the worry. Advice like "no coffee after lunch" does not touch that loop. CBT-I attacks it directly — by restricting time in bed, restoring stimulus control, and challenging the beliefs that keep the alarm system on.
</Callout>

## When it is not sleep hygiene: CBT-I

Chronic insomnia disorder means difficulty falling or staying asleep on three or more nights a week for three months or longer, together with daytime consequences such as fatigue, irritability or poor concentration.

Every current guideline — the AASM, the American College of Physicians, and the European insomnia guideline — names **cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I)** as first-line treatment, ahead of both sleeping pills and sleep hygiene education.

CBT-I is a structured programme, usually four to eight sessions, combining:

- **sleep restriction** — compressing time in bed towards actual sleep time, then widening it gradually;
- **stimulus control** — rebuilding the bed-equals-sleep association;
- **cognitive work** — on catastrophic beliefs such as "if I do not fall asleep now, tomorrow is ruined";
- **relaxation training**, with sleep hygiene included at the end as a maintenance layer rather than the treatment itself.

Its short-term effect matches that of hypnotics, and its long-term effect exceeds them because the gains persist after therapy ends. Digital CBT-I programmes with demonstrated efficacy now exist, which largely solves the old problem of finding a trained therapist.

<Callout type="warning" title="See a doctor rather than adjusting your routine">
  Seek medical assessment if any of the following apply: loud snoring with pauses in breathing or gasping that a partner has noticed; waking up choking or short of breath; heavy daytime sleepiness, especially falling asleep at the wheel or at your desk; an irresistible urge to move your legs in the evening; morning headaches or raised blood pressure alongside poor sleep. These can indicate obstructive sleep apnoea, restless legs syndrome or narcolepsy — conditions that no bedtime routine will fix and that need proper diagnosis. Sleepiness while driving is an immediate reason to stop driving until the cause is identified.
</Callout>

## Where to start

1. **Pick a wake time** you can genuinely hold on a Saturday. Keep it for two weeks and change nothing else — that alone usually reveals how much of the problem was schedule drift.
2. **Audit the bedroom once**: temperature, full darkness, no glowing indicators. A one-off fix with a permanent payoff.
3. **Move your caffeine cut-off** to midday. If your intake is high, taper rather than quitting outright, or expect two or three days of headache.
4. **Take alcohol out of the evening** for a fortnight and compare how mornings feel.
5. **Adopt the 20-minute rule.** Not asleep, not staying in bed.
6. **Reassess after a month.** If you are still struggling three or more nights a week, the answer is not more discipline — it is CBT-I.

Sleep hygiene is the foundation, not the treatment. It makes good sleep more likely and removes the obvious obstacles, but it will not dismantle a disorder that has already established itself. Knowing where that line sits saves months of well-intentioned effort aimed at the wrong target.
