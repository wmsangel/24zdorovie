Jet lag is a mismatch between your body clock and the new local time after a fast flight across several time zones. Your body is still on the old time while everything around you is on a new one: you feel sleepy by day, cannot sleep at night, and add grogginess and digestive upset. The reset runs at roughly one day per zone crossed, but you can speed it up — with light, melatonin and schedule. And yes, flying east is objectively harder.

## What happens to the clock

The brain has a "master clock" that synchronises the whole body with the day–night cycle. On a fast flight across several zones, external time changes abruptly while the body clock changes slowly. A gap opens: the body releases melatonin and drops its temperature "at the wrong time", and sleep and wakefulness no longer match the local schedule.

This is exactly why a short one- or two-zone flight is barely felt, while a transatlantic or transcontinental one knocks you out for several days.

<Callout type="science" title="Why east is trickier">
  The human body clock averages slightly more than 24 hours, so it is more natural for the body to "stretch" the day — going to bed and waking later. Flying west does exactly that and is easier to handle. Flying east forces you to live earlier than your internal time, which the clock adapts to more slowly.
</Callout>

**Light** is the main tool. The general principle: flying east, catch bright morning light and avoid early-evening light; flying west, the opposite. The exact "window" depends on the number of zones, and for long flights it is handy to check a ready-made schedule.

**Melatonin** taken at destination bedtime helps both shift the rhythm and fall asleep. It is one of the few methods with good evidence specifically for jet lag. The timing matters more than the dose.

## What to do in practice

- **2–4 days before the trip**, gradually shift your sleep time toward the new zone: earlier before an eastward flight, later before a westward one.
- **On the plane**, set your watch to the destination time right away and, if possible, sleep on the new schedule rather than the old one.
- **On arrival**, actively manage light (seek or avoid it at the right hours) and stick to the local routine — eat and sleep on the new time, even if it takes effort.
- **Caffeine** can help alertness in the local morning, but avoid it in the second half of the day so it does not disrupt night sleep.

You cannot fully avoid jet lag on a long flight, but managing light and schedule well cuts it several-fold. The key is not to "wait it out" but to actively adjust your clock to the new time from day one.
