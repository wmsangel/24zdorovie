The gut microbiome is a community of tens of trillions of microorganisms living mostly in your large intestine. Their combined genome is hundreds of times larger than our own, and they do what our cells cannot: break down fiber and turn it into compounds that affect digestion, immunity and metabolism. Caring for the microbiome is simpler than it sounds — the main lever is not supplements or tests, but the variety of plant foods you eat and getting enough fiber.

## What the microbiome is and where it lives

The human body hosts around 38 trillion bacteria — roughly the same number as our own cells. Most of them sit in the large intestine, where near-oxygen-free conditions suit anaerobic microbes perfectly. There is also a microbial community in the mouth, on the skin and in the airways, but it is the gut community that people mean by "the microbiome": it is the largest and the best studied.

Two terms are worth separating. **Microbiota** refers to the organisms themselves: bacteria, plus viruses (mostly bacteriophages), fungi and archaea. **Microbiome** is the microbiota together with their genes and metabolic products. In everyday use the words are treated as synonyms, and that is fine.

The collective genome of gut bacteria carries hundreds of times more genes than the human genome. Those genes encode enzymes we simply do not have. We cannot break down most dietary fibers, for instance — but hundreds of bacterial species can, and do it on our behalf. In effect we have outsourced part of digestion to microbes, and in return we give them a stable home and food.

Each person's microbiota is as individual as a fingerprint. Two healthy people can carry radically different species while both have perfectly normal metabolism. That is why the field increasingly looks not at specific "good" and "bad" bacteria, but at the functions of the community as a whole — and at its diversity.

## Short-chain fatty acids: the microbiome's main product

When fiber reaches the large intestine intact, bacteria ferment it and produce **short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)** — chiefly butyrate, propionate and acetate. This is the main currency the microbiome pays back to its host in exchange for food.

- **Butyrate** is the primary fuel for the cells lining the colon (colonocytes). It also helps maintain the integrity of the gut barrier and regulates local inflammation.
- **Propionate** is absorbed into the blood, reaches the liver and takes part in glucose and lipid metabolism.
- **Acetate** is the most abundant; it influences appetite and serves as a substrate for other metabolic processes.

Mechanistically, SCFAs are how researchers explain many of the perks of a high-fiber diet — from steadier blood sugar to anti-inflammatory effects. A review in the journal Cell lays out in detail how these molecules connect what we eat to whole-body physiology. One important caveat: much of this evidence comes from animal and lab models and should be extrapolated to humans with care. But the overall direction — "more fiber → more substrate for SCFAs" — holds up in human studies too.

<Callout type="science" title="Why this is unusual from a nutrition angle">
  There is a paradox here: we eat something we cannot absorb ourselves (fiber) for the sake of what our own bacteria produce from it. So it matters not only how much fiber you get but how varied it is — different fibers feed different groups of microbes.
</Callout>

## Diversity is the key metric, not the "right" bacteria

If microbiome health has one near-universal marker, it is **diversity**. The more different bacterial species coexist in the gut, and the more evenly they are represented, the more resilient the community: an illness, a course of antibiotics or food poisoning is easier to weather when there is someone to pick up the functions of species that drop out.

Low microbiota diversity is linked in observational studies to obesity, type 2 diabetes, inflammatory bowel disease and other conditions. Note the causation caveat: a link does not mean a poor microbiome causes these diseases — often it is the other way round, or a third factor (such as the diet itself) drives both. But as a target, diversity is convenient: you can raise it with clear actions, and there is no downside to doing so.

And microbiota diversity grows above all from diet diversity. Different plants contain different types of fiber and polyphenols, and each type of substrate supports its own set of bacteria. A monotonous diet — even a nominally "healthy" one — feeds a narrow circle of species.

## "30 plants a week": where the number comes from

The most practical benchmark came from the **American Gut Project** — the largest crowdsourced microbiome study, published in the journal mSystems in 2018. Researchers collected samples from more than 10,000 participants and matched microbiota composition against their dietary questionnaires.

The key finding: people who ate **more than 30 different plant foods per week** had a markedly more diverse microbiome than those who stuck to 10 types or fewer. The number of *different* plants turned out to be a stronger predictor of diversity than formal labels like "vegan" or "meat-eater."

What matters about this figure:

- **30 is a benchmark, not a threshold.** There is nothing magic about the number; the point is the direction "eat more variety," not hitting exactly 30.
- **Almost anything plant-based counts.** Vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes, nuts, seeds — plus herbs and spices (with less "weight," but they still count).
- **Different colors and groups beat volume.** Five apples are one plant; an apple, a pear, a handful of oats, some beans and a pinch of thyme are already five.

American Gut is observational — it shows a link, it does not prove a cause. But "eat more different plants" is safe, cheap and consistent with every other dietary recommendation, so you can act on it with confidence.

## Fiber and fermented foods: what works

The two actions with the strongest evidence are **more fiber** and **regular fermented foods**.

### Fiber

Fiber is food for the microbiota (much of it is classed scientifically as a prebiotic). The WHO recommends at least 25 g of fiber per day, and it is at this level that studies record reduced risk of cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes. Most adults eat about half that. Different fiber types — inulin from onions and chicory, beta-glucans from oats, pectins from fruit, resistant starch from cooled potatoes and rice — feed different bacteria, so it pays to rotate your sources.

### Fermented foods

Yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut, kimchi, kombucha and some cheeses contain live microorganisms and fermentation products. A review in Nutrients (2022) describes how fermented foods can raise microbiota diversity and lower inflammatory markers. One small but well-run study from a Stanford team deserves a mention: people who ate more fermented foods for 10 weeks saw their microbiota diversity rise and inflammatory markers fall — an effect even more pronounced than in the group that loaded up on fiber alone. The sample was small, so treat this as a promising signal rather than a final verdict.

### What about probiotics in capsules?

Here the honest answer is "it depends." Probiotic supplements are specific strains at specific doses, and they work narrowly: there is decent evidence for preventing antibiotic-associated diarrhea and for some forms of irritable bowel syndrome. But the idea that any jar of probiotics "improves the microbiome" in a healthy person is not supported by evidence. Strain, dose and indication are everything.

## What does NOT work: expensive tests, and being honest about the gaps

The industry around the microbiome has grown faster than the science. Two things deserve a sober look.

**Commercial stool microbiome tests.** For a hefty fee you get a colorful report: this many "good" bacteria, this many "bad," personalized food advice. The problem is that science does not yet know what an ideal microbiome looks like — there is no reference standard. The same sample can yield different results across labs, and the final advice almost always boils down to generic "eat more fiber and different plants," which is true without any test. For a healthy person, this is a premature expense. (Clinically ordered tests are a different matter — a *C. difficile* test or colorectal cancer screening, for example, is a separate story with its own evidence.)

**Promises to "reset" your microbiome with a course.** Detox powders, "cleanses" and challenges that promise a new microbiome in 7 days sell a feeling of control, not a result. The microbiome does respond to food within a day or two, but a durable shift is a matter of weeks of consistent eating, not a one-off course.

<Callout type="warning" title="Honest note on the evidence gap">
  The microbiome is a young field. Most of the loud claims ("this bacterium → this disease") come from observational studies or mice and do not prove causation. We cannot yet deliberately "engineer" a healthy microbiome. So the only reliable levers are the basic, safe ones — diet, movement, sleep — not personalized schemes from a commercial report.
</Callout>

## What else shapes the microbiome

Diet is the biggest factor, but not the only one.

- **Antibiotics** sharply reduce diversity; recovery takes weeks, sometimes months. Take them strictly as prescribed — but do not refuse them out of fear for your microbiome when a doctor has prescribed them.
- **Movement.** Regular physical activity is associated in studies with greater microbiota diversity, independent of diet.
- **Sleep and stress.** The gut–brain axis runs both ways: chronic stress and poor sleep alter the microbiota, which in turn affects mood and inflammation.
- **Early life.** Mode of birth, breastfeeding and the first years of life set the baseline microbiota profile — but adult habits can and should adjust it.

## What to do: the practical minimum

1. **Count plants, not grams of fiber.** Aim for 30 different plant foods per week. Build the habit of adding at least one new type each week.
2. **Get fiber up to 25–30 g per day.** Legumes, whole grains, vegetables, berries. Raise your intake gradually, about +5 g per week, and drink enough water — otherwise, bloating.
3. **Add something fermented every day.** A serving of yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut or kimchi. Choose "live cultures" options without added sugar.
4. **Move and sleep well.** These affect the microbiome no less than individual foods.
5. **Do not spend money on a microbiome test** — for now it will tell you nothing this list does not.
6. **Antibiotics only as prescribed.** After a course, lean on fiber and fermented foods to aid recovery.

The microbiome is not an organ you can steer with a pill, but an ecosystem you cultivate with what goes on your plate every day. The good news is that the recipe for it is exactly the same as for the heart, weight and metabolism: more different plants, more fiber, less ultra-processed food. If you have concerns about digestion or specific symptoms, discuss them with a doctor — not with a lab report.
