Probiotics are live beneficial bacteria; prebiotics are the fibers those bacteria feed on. The distinction is fundamental: one adds microbes, the other feeds the ones you already have. And a probiotic's effect depends heavily on the strain and the goal — "for gut flora in general" achieves almost nothing. For most healthy people, varied fiber and fermented food do more than a jar of supplement.

## Two different concepts

The words sound alike, so they get confused, but the mechanisms are opposite.

- **Probiotics** are live microorganisms that, in sufficient amounts, confer a benefit. These are supplements with specific strains and fermented foods: yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut, kimchi.
- **Prebiotics** are food components we cannot digest but that feed beneficial bacteria. In essence they are certain fiber types: inulin, fructo-oligosaccharides (FOS), galacto-oligosaccharides (GOS).

There are derived terms too: **synbiotics** combine both, and **postbiotics** are the beneficial products of bacterial activity. But the foundation is these two categories.

<Callout type="info" title="A simple analogy">
  Probiotics are planting new plants in a garden. Prebiotics are fertilising the soil so what is already planted grows better. For a resilient garden, feeding the soil is usually more reliable than a one-off planting.
</Callout>

## What the research says about probiotics

The main mistake is treating probiotics as a single product. Their effect is **strain-specific**: data on one strain say nothing about another, even of the same species. So the correct question is not "do probiotics work" but "does this strain work for this condition".

One important nuance: there is evidence that routinely taking probiotics after a course of antibiotics may **slow** the recovery of your own microbiome rather than speed it. That is another reason not to take them "just in case".

## Prebiotics: this is just fiber

The good news: prebiotics do not need to be bought separately. They are a type of dietary fiber, and the best way to get them is varied plant food. Legumes, whole grains, vegetables, fruit, nuts, onion, garlic and bananas all feed beneficial bacteria.

The mechanism is specific: fermenting these fibers, bacteria produce short-chain fatty acids (butyrate above all), which feed the gut lining and reduce inflammation. This is exactly why the gap between the fiber target and what people actually eat matters more than the choice of any supplement.

## What to do in practice

For most healthy people the strategy is simple: **food first**. Varied fiber feeds your own bacteria, fermented foods provide live cultures — all without inflated promises or extra cost.

A probiotic supplement is justified selectively: for a specific goal, a specific strain with a proven effect and, ideally, on a doctor's advice — for example, certain strains during a course of antibiotics. A "probiotic for gut flora" with no goal and no named strain is more often marketing than medicine.
