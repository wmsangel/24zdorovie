If you are a healthy adult eating a reasonably varied diet, a daily multivitamin will almost certainly do nothing measurable for you: it does not lower overall mortality, does not prevent heart attacks or strokes, and does not reduce cancer incidence. That is the conclusion of the US Preventive Services Task Force in 2022 and of the systematic review underpinning it. But the honest version of this story has a second half, because several groups of people genuinely do need specific supplements, and for one of them — anyone who might become pregnant — the recommendation is not optional.

## What the large trials actually found

In June 2022 the USPSTF updated its guidance on vitamin and mineral supplements for the primary prevention of cardiovascular disease and cancer. The evidence base was a systematic review by O'Connor and colleagues, published in the same issue of JAMA: 84 studies, 78 of them randomized controlled trials, together covering hundreds of thousands of participants.

For multivitamins the picture was flat. A couple of large trials hinted at a small reduction in cancer incidence, but the signal was inconsistent across studies and was not accompanied by any reduction in cancer mortality, cardiovascular events or all-cause death. The Task Force's formal wording is that the evidence is insufficient to weigh benefits against harms — an "I" statement, not a green light.

Two nutrients got a firmer verdict. The USPSTF recommends **against** beta-carotene and vitamin E for preventing cardiovascular disease or cancer. Vitamin E showed no benefit; beta-carotene showed active harm. That is a grade D recommendation, which is unusual — a public body saying "do not do this" rather than "we do not know".

The scope matters as much as the finding. These statements apply to community-dwelling, non-pregnant adults without a known deficiency. They do not override treatment of diagnosed deficiencies, do not apply to people with malabsorption, and sit alongside a completely separate USPSTF recommendation on folic acid for anyone planning a pregnancy.

Why the null result is not surprising: multivitamins were invented as insurance against deficiency diseases — scurvy, beriberi, pellagra. In countries with fortified flour, iodized salt and year-round produce, those diseases mostly vanished while the pill stayed. Curing a deficiency and improving the health of someone who has none are simply different problems, and the second one turned out to be much harder.

The first row deserves emphasis because it outweighs everything else on the list. Taking 400–800 mcg of folic acid before conception and through early pregnancy reduces the risk of neural tube defects such as anencephaly and spina bifida. It carries a grade A recommendation — not "consider it", but "everyone who could become pregnant should take it". The timing is the whole point: by the time a test reads positive, the critical window has largely closed.

<Callout type="info" title="B12 for vegans is not a debate">
  Vitamin B12 is made by bacteria and reaches humans through animal foods. Plant sources do not supply it in usable form: algae and fermented soy contain analogues that occupy the same receptors without doing the job, which can make matters worse rather than better. The liver holds several years' worth, so deficiency creeps in over three to five years — fatigue and tingling in the fingers first, then damage to the dorsal columns of the spinal cord that often does not fully recover. A standard regimen is 50–100 mcg of cyanocobalamin daily or 1000–2000 mcg weekly; fortified foods count toward the total.
</Callout>

## Why "just in case" is a poor strategy

"It can't hurt" sounds harmless. It has three specific problems.

**Vitamins have ceilings.** Most nutrients have a tolerable upper intake level, and a single multivitamin rarely comes close to it. Trouble starts when someone takes a multivitamin plus a separate vitamin D, plus zinc "for immunity", plus fortified cereal and protein bars. Nobody adds these up.

**Some effects are not merely null but negative.** This is not a hypothetical; it comes from large randomized trials that were designed to demonstrate benefit and found the opposite.

**A pill substitutes for the things that matter.** Swallowing something feels like taking action and relieves the anxiety, while smoking, sleep, movement and diet stay untouched. That displacement effect is hard to measure but real.

<Callout type="warning" title="Beta-carotene in smokers, and vitamin A in pregnancy">
  Two trials permanently changed how antioxidant supplements are viewed. The Finnish **ATBC** study (N Engl J Med, 1994) randomized more than 29,000 male smokers to vitamin E, beta-carotene, both or placebo. In the beta-carotene arm, lung cancer incidence was **18% higher** and overall mortality 8% higher. The American **CARET** trial (Omenn GS et al., N Engl J Med, 1996) tested beta-carotene plus retinol in smokers and asbestos-exposed workers and was halted early: 28% more lung cancer and 17% higher overall mortality. If you smoke, or quit recently, beta-carotene supplements are contraindicated — and any "smoker's formula" loaded with antioxidants is marketing that contradicts the data.

  The second warning is just as firm: **retinol in pregnancy**. High-dose preformed vitamin A is teratogenic, with documented craniofacial, cardiac and central nervous system malformations. The adult upper limit is 3000 mcg RAE (10,000 IU) of preformed vitamin A per day, and pregnancy is no place to approach it. This is exactly why prenatal formulas supply vitamin A mainly as beta-carotene, which the body converts to retinol under regulation and does not accumulate to toxic levels. A standard adult multivitamin containing retinol is not a substitute for a prenatal one, and isotretinoin and related retinoids are absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy.
</Callout>

### Other places it is easy to overshoot

- **Iron.** The body has no active route for excreting iron. Supplementing without a confirmed deficiency drives overload, which is particularly dangerous in hereditary haemochromatosis — roughly 1 in 200 people of European descent. Men and postmenopausal women should not take iron without a ferritin result.
- **Selenium.** A narrow window: 55 mcg a day is the requirement, 400 mcg the upper limit. Chronic excess causes selenosis — brittle hair and nails, a garlic odour on the breath, peripheral neuropathy.
- **Vitamin D.** Safe at sensible doses, but repeated 50,000 IU regimens taken without checking 25(OH)D can produce hypercalcaemia and kidney damage.
- **Zinc.** Above 40 mg a day long term it interferes with copper absorption, eventually causing anaemia and neurological symptoms.
- **Vitamin E.** No benefit in the USPSTF review, and separate meta-analyses have linked high doses to increased all-cause mortality and haemorrhagic stroke.

## Expensive urine: where the joke holds and where it fails

"Vitamins are just expensive urine" is exactly half right, and the boundary is more useful than the punchline.

**True for water-soluble vitamins.** Vitamin C and the B vitamins are barely stored. Once plasma concentration exceeds the renal threshold, the surplus leaves in urine. The bright yellow colour after a multivitamin is riboflavin (B2) — a harmless dye and an honest indicator that part of the dose passed straight through. Vitamin C above roughly 400 mg a day is largely excreted, and doses from 2 g upward cause diarrhoea and raise the risk of oxalate stones.

**False for fat-soluble vitamins and minerals.** A, D, E and K accumulate in liver and adipose tissue. Iron, copper, selenium and zinc are stored too. For this group, excess is not wasted money but a pharmacological load with real consequences — which is why upper limits exist for them and are worth respecting.

<Callout type="science" title="COSMOS: how to read a positive result carefully">
  COSMOS — the COcoa Supplement and Multivitamin Outcomes Study — is a large randomized trial with more than 21,000 participants. On its primary endpoints, cardiovascular events and cancer, the multivitamin arm showed no significant advantage. The interesting signals came from its cognitive sub-studies: in COSMOS-Web (Yeung LK et al., Am J Clin Nutr, 2023), older adults on the multivitamin performed better on an immediate word-recall task, an effect the authors characterised as roughly equivalent to three years of age-related memory decline.

  Four reasons not to treat that as a recommendation. It is a secondary analysis in a sub-cohort, not the trial's pre-specified primary aim. The testing was done online, without in-person neuropsychological assessment. "Better word recall" is not the same outcome as "less dementia", and none of the COSMOS cognitive work demonstrated an effect on dementia incidence. And the participants were mostly older, well-educated Americans, so extrapolating to a 35-year-old has no basis. The direction is worth further study; it is a hypothesis, not guidance.
</Callout>

## Product quality and what the label means

There is a regulatory layer to this too. In the United States, supplements fall under the 1994 DSHEA act: manufacturers do not have to demonstrate efficacy or safety to the FDA before a product goes on sale, and the agency mostly acts after problems appear. Most other jurisdictions treat supplements as food rather than medicine on similar terms.

Independent testing shows what follows. Analyses routinely find gaps between labelled and actual content in both directions — under-dosed active ingredients as often as overages. The worst category is sports and weight-loss formulations, where undeclared pharmaceutical compounds have repeatedly been detected.

<Callout type="tip" title="How to choose, if you do need one">
  Look for third-party verification marks — USP Verified, NSF Certified for Sport, Informed Choice. They do not prove the product works; they confirm the bottle contains what the label claims. Be sceptical of percentages: 500–1000% of the daily value for water-soluble vitamins is a marketing device, not a benefit. Check the form of vitamin A if you are of reproductive age — beta-carotene, not retinyl palmitate. Check whether iron is included, and choose an iron-free formula if you have no documented need. And add up everything: multivitamin plus standalone supplements plus fortified foods share one ceiling.
</Callout>

## What to do in practice

1. **Start with food, not the pharmacy shelf.** Vegetables and fruit, whole grains, legumes, fish, nuts and dairy or fortified alternatives cover nearly every nutrient requirement.
2. **Check whether you fall into an indicated group.** Pregnancy or planning one, a vegan diet, bariatric surgery, malabsorption, age over 70, long-term metformin or PPIs, an intake below 1500 kcal.
3. **Do not self-prescribe iron or preformed vitamin A.** Iron follows a ferritin result; vitamin A in pregnancy follows a clinician and the form on the label.
4. **If you smoke or quit recently, avoid beta-carotene supplements entirely.** This is the one item with direct trial evidence of harm.
5. **Add up doses across all sources.** The upper limit applies to the total, not to each bottle separately.
6. **Test rather than guess when symptoms appear.** Fatigue and hair loss can be anaemia, thyroid disease, B12 or vitamin D deficiency — no multivitamin name tells you which.

Multivitamins are neither poison nor panacea. For a healthy person eating well, they are a modest waste of money carrying a small but non-zero risk in particular circumstances. For someone pregnant, vegan or post-gastric surgery, they are a necessary part of staying well. The useful question is never "do vitamins work" but whether there is a specific reason for you to take a specific nutrient.
