Ashwagandha is a plant adaptogen surrounded by many promises. Separating the proven from the marketing: its best-studied effect is on **stress, anxiety and sleep** — here there are several decent, if small, studies. For testosterone, strength and "energy in general", the evidence is weaker and more conflicting. It is not a drug or a cure-all, but one of the few supplements in this category with any evidence base at all.

## Where the evidence is strongest: stress and anxiety

Ashwagandha is classed as an adaptogen — plants credited with increasing resilience to stress. Unlike many of its shelf-mates, it has human studies. In several randomised, placebo-controlled trials, a standardised root extract reduced subjective stress and anxiety, and sometimes cortisol.

That is the best thing to say about the supplement: not "ancient wisdom" but a measurable effect under controlled conditions. The honest caveat: the studies are small and short, often funded by manufacturers, so this is "helps many" rather than a guarantee.

<Callout type="science" title="What 'standardised extract' means">
  The effect in studies came not from root powder in general but from an extract with a fixed content of active compounds (withanolides). A cheap supplement with no stated standardisation has an unpredictable content of active components — and an unpredictable result.
</Callout>

## Sleep and recovery

The second most reliable area is sleep. There is evidence that ashwagandha slightly improves sleep quality and makes falling asleep easier, more noticeably in people with stress and insomnia. The effect is moderate: it is not a sleeping pill but gentle support. It makes sense to consider it alongside sleep hygiene, not as a replacement.

Be especially cautious with claims about testosterone and muscle gain: a few small studies showed something, but the quality of evidence is low, and it is not worth betting on.

## Safety and who should not take it

For most healthy adults, short courses are reasonably well tolerated. But there are important limits: ashwagandha is not recommended in pregnancy, and is used with caution in thyroid disease (it may affect thyroid hormones) and autoimmune conditions. Rare cases of liver injury have been reported. If you take medication or have chronic conditions, discuss it with a doctor before starting.

<Callout type="warning" title="A necessary note">
  Ashwagandha is a dietary supplement, not a medicine. It does not replace treatment for anxiety disorders, depression or insomnia. Persistent symptoms need a professional's help, not self-treatment with supplements.
</Callout>

The bottom line: ashwagandha is one of the few adaptogens with real, if modest, evidence for stress and sleep. It is reasonable to consider as a gentle addition to stress management and routine, to choose a standardised extract, and not to expect effects the studies do not support.
