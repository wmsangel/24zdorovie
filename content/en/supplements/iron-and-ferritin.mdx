Iron deficiency begins long before haemoglobin drops, and ferritin is the first marker to fall, which is why a normal full blood count proves nothing on its own. Ferritin has one big catch: it is an acute phase protein, so any inflammation pushes it up and makes a real deficiency look normal, which is why it is read together with CRP. And the part that matters most: iron is not a supplement to take speculatively, because the body cannot excrete the excess and because accidental overdose of iron products is still a leading cause of fatal poisoning in young children.

## Deficiency and anaemia are not the same thing

The most common misreading of a blood test goes like this: haemoglobin is normal, therefore iron is fine. It is not. Iron deficiency develops in stages, and anaemia is only the last one.

An adult body holds roughly 3 to 4 grams of iron. Most of it sits in haemoglobin, a good share in muscle myoglobin, and the rest is stored as ferritin and haemosiderin in the liver, spleen and bone marrow. That store is the buffer the body spends first when intake stops covering losses.

Hence three stages:

1. **Store depletion.** Ferritin falls and marrow iron drops. Haemoglobin is still normal, but symptoms may already be present: fatigue, poor exercise tolerance, hair shedding, brittle nails, trouble concentrating, sometimes restless legs.
2. **Iron deficient erythropoiesis.** The store is gone and transport iron runs short. Transferrin and total iron binding capacity rise, transferrin saturation falls. Haemoglobin may still scrape the bottom of the reference range.
3. **Iron deficiency anaemia.** Haemoglobin finally drops and red cells become small and pale, with low MCV and MCH.

The practical consequence is that there is a long window between "everything is fine" and "anaemia" during which someone feels genuinely unwell and a routine full blood count shows nothing. You catch that window with ferritin, not with haemoglobin.

## Ferritin: the best marker, and its trap

Serum ferritin is the most efficient and cost effective test for diagnosing iron deficiency, because it reflects the size of the store and drops during the very first stage of depletion. The 2021 British Society of Gastroenterology guideline calls it the single most useful marker of iron deficiency anaemia.

And that is exactly where the trap sits. Ferritin is also an acute phase protein. It rises with infection, active inflammation, autoimmune disease, obesity, fatty liver, heavy alcohol use and malignancy. In a person with inflammation, a ferritin of 60 mcg/L may not mean "adequate stores" — it may mean "empty stores plus inflammation".

<Callout type="science" title="Why ferritin is read together with CRP">
  The meta-analysis underpinning the WHO guideline found that inflammation raises ferritin by roughly 30% on average and leads to underestimating iron deficiency by about 14%. WHO therefore recommends measuring ferritin alongside inflammation markers — C-reactive protein and alpha-1-acid glycoprotein — and then either excluding people with raised CRP, applying correction factors, or simply raising the cut-off that defines deficiency. The second way around the trap is transferrin saturation: in true iron deficiency it is low even when ferritin looks respectable, whereas in anaemia of inflammation the iron is physically present but locked away by hepcidin.
</Callout>

One caveat in the other direction: a high ferritin is not a diagnosis of iron overload. WHO explicitly says ferritin should never be used alone to identify overload, because inflammation, obesity, fatty liver disease and alcohol explain far more high results than haemochromatosis does. A raised ferritin is a reason to investigate, not a reason to start donating blood.

## Who is most likely to run low

Iron is lost with blood, so the risk groups are the people who lose blood regularly and the people who replace it poorly.

- **Menstruating women.** Heavy or prolonged periods are the leading cause of deficiency in this group. The RDA for women aged 19 to 50 is 18 mg a day, against 8 mg for men of the same age.
- **Pregnancy.** Requirements rise to 27 mg a day to cover expanded blood volume, the placenta and the fetus.
- **Vegetarians and vegans.** Their requirement is set 1.8 times higher, because non-haem iron from plants is far less bioavailable.
- **Blood donors.** Every donation removes roughly 200 to 250 mg of iron, and frequent donors often do not rebuild stores between sessions.
- **Endurance athletes.** Losses through sweat and urine, foot-strike capillary damage in runners, higher turnover and frequently under-fuelled diets all stack up.
- **Infants, toddlers and adolescents.** Rapid growth and expanding blood volume, plus the onset of menstruation in teenage girls.
- **People with gut disease.** Coeliac disease is found in 3–5% of iron deficiency anaemia cases; inflammatory bowel disease, gastric surgery, Helicobacter pylori, long-term NSAIDs and proton pump inhibitors all contribute.

## New deficiency in men and postmenopausal women is a red flag

This is the part that must not be skipped, and it matters more than which brand of tablet you buy.

In a woman of reproductive age, iron deficiency usually has an obvious explanation: menstrual loss. In a man, or in a woman past menopause, there is no physiological route for losing blood. The iron is going somewhere — and most often it is going into the gastrointestinal tract.

<Callout type="warning" title="Iron deficiency can be the first sign of a tumour">
  The British Society of Gastroenterology reports that roughly a third of men and postmenopausal women presenting with iron deficiency anaemia turn out to have underlying pathology, most commonly in the GI tract. Iron deficiency anaemia can be the first and only presenting sign of colonic or oesophago-gastric cancer, often with no abdominal symptoms whatsoever. The guideline therefore recommends gastroscopy and colonoscopy as first-line investigations in men and postmenopausal women with newly diagnosed iron deficiency anaemia, along with serological screening for coeliac disease. Iron replacement should not be delayed while waiting for those tests — but it must not replace them either. Quietly raising your ferritin with a supplement and considering the matter closed means you have suppressed a symptom, not solved a problem.
</Callout>

In young women the picture is gentler: significant gut pathology is uncommon, and after coeliac screening, further investigation is warranted only if there are additional concerning features. Confirmed deficiency in young men is rare, but when it occurs it deserves the same work-up as in older adults.

## Haem and non-haem iron

Dietary iron comes in two forms. Haem iron is found in meat, poultry and seafood, absorbs well, and is relatively indifferent to what you eat alongside it. Non-haem iron is present in plants, fortified foods and also in meat; it absorbs poorly and is highly sensitive to the rest of the meal.

Overall bioavailability is around 14–18% from a mixed diet containing meat, seafood and vitamin C, and 5–12% from vegetarian diets. That gap is exactly why the vegetarian requirement carries a 1.8 multiplier.

Spinach deserves a note. Popular culture made it the iron food, but its iron is non-haem and it comes packaged with polyphenols and oxalates that bind it, so real-world bioavailability is low. Red meat, liver, shellfish, and legumes eaten with a vitamin C source do far more for your ferritin.

## What helps and what blocks absorption

These effects are real and well characterised for non-haem iron, although a typical Western diet blunts them somewhat.

**Absorption enhancers:**

- Ascorbic acid, by a wide margin the strongest. Peppers, citrus, kiwi, berries, sauerkraut — or, with a supplement, a glass of orange juice or 100–200 mg of vitamin C.
- Meat, poultry and fish, which enhance non-haem iron absorption from plant foods eaten in the same meal.
- Soaking, sprouting and fermenting grains and legumes, which break down some of the phytate.

**Absorption inhibitors:**

- **Phytate** in whole grains, bran and legumes.
- **Polyphenols** in tea, coffee, cocoa and red wine. A mug of black tea with a meal measurably cuts iron uptake, so separate them by an hour or more.
- **Calcium**, the one inhibitor that interferes with both non-haem and haem iron. Take dairy and calcium supplements away from iron.
- **Reduced stomach acid.** Proton pump inhibitors reduce non-haem iron absorption. In people with normal stores, even a decade of PPI use is not associated with iron depletion, but someone who is already deficient may respond poorly to oral iron while taking them.

## How to take iron: the change of the last decade

The traditional advice — a tablet two or three times a day — turned out to be worse than the alternative, and the reason is a hormone called hepcidin.

Hepcidin is the master regulator of iron metabolism. Made in the liver, it blocks the export of iron from intestinal cells into the bloodstream. Every dose of iron raises hepcidin, and the rise lasts around 24 hours. A second tablet later the same day, or a tablet the next morning, arrives at a gate that is already closed.

<Callout type="tip" title="The regimen that actually absorbs better">
  In the trial by Stoffel and colleagues (Lancet Haematology, 2017), iron-depleted women took 60 mg of iron either on consecutive days or on alternate days. Cumulative fractional absorption was 21.8% with alternate day dosing versus 16.3% with daily dosing, and total absorbed iron was 175 mg versus 131 mg — from half as many tablets. In the second arm of the study, splitting the daily dose into two did not improve absorption but did raise hepcidin further. The 2020 follow-up in Haematologica confirmed the effect in women who already had iron deficiency anaemia: fractional absorption on alternate days was 40–50% higher. The practical translation is simple. One dose, in the morning, on an empty stomach, every other day, washed down with water or a vitamin C source, and never with tea, coffee or milk.
</Callout>

The BSG guideline recommends starting with one tablet a day of ferrous sulphate, fumarate or gluconate, and switching to one tablet every other day if that is not tolerated. Response is checked at four weeks; a haemoglobin rise of 10 g/L or more within two weeks is considered a good answer. Treatment then continues for about three months after haemoglobin normalises, in order to refill the stores rather than just fix the number. The logic is that haemoglobin recovers faster than ferritin, so stopping the moment the count looks fine guarantees a relapse.

## Side effects and how to reduce them

Doses of 45 mg of elemental iron a day and above frequently cause gastrointestinal effects: nausea, abdominal discomfort, constipation, sometimes diarrhoea. Stools turn dark in almost everyone, which is expected and not a sign of bleeding.

What helps:

- Move to alternate day dosing. Tolerability improves and absorption does not suffer.
- Reduce the single dose instead of abandoning treatment altogether.
- Take it with a small amount of food if fasting is intolerable. Absorption drops a little, but consistency matters more than perfection.
- Ask about gentler preparations: amino acid chelates, polysaccharide-iron complexes and carbonyl iron tend to cause fewer GI effects than ferrous salts.
- If oral iron fails or cannot be tolerated, intravenous iron is an option — a clinical decision, not a self-directed one.

## Why "just in case" is the wrong approach to iron

Iron differs from most vitamins in one decisive way: humans have no active route for excreting it. Only absorption is regulated. Whatever gets in beyond need is deposited in tissue — liver, heart, pancreas, joints.

<Callout type="danger" title="Three reasons never to take iron without testing">
  **Children.** The FDA requires iron-containing supplements sold in solid form to carry a warning that accidental overdose of iron-containing products is a leading cause of fatal poisoning in children under 6. Between 1983 and 2000 at least 43 US children died after ingesting high-dose iron supplements, and accidental iron ingestion accounted for around a third of reported childhood poisoning deaths between 1983 and 1991. Store iron where children cannot reach it, and never give iron to a child without a clinician's instruction.

  **Haemochromatosis.** This inherited condition makes the gut absorb more iron than the body needs. Untreated, it produces cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, cardiac disease and pancreatic damage, typically by the fourth decade. People with it are advised to avoid both iron and vitamin C supplements — and many do not know they have it until they are tested.

  **Treating a symptom instead of finding the cause.** Fatigue has dozens of explanations. Taking iron blindly either does nothing or masks blood loss that needed to be found. In men and postmenopausal women, that lost time can be the difference in a gastrointestinal cancer.
</Callout>

The Tolerable Upper Intake Level for iron is 45 mg a day for adults and 40 mg for children up to 13. That figure applies to people without deficiency; therapeutic doses for confirmed anaemia are higher and are prescribed. Overload from food alone is essentially unheard of in people with normal gut function — it is supplements and prescriptions that create the risk.

A word on iron and tiredness. The 2016 Cochrane review of daily iron supplementation in menstruating women pooled 67 trials and 8,506 participants and confirmed that iron reliably reduces the risk of anaemia and raises haemoglobin. That is evidence about correcting a deficiency, not about boosting energy in people who have adequate stores. If your ferritin is normal, iron will not make you feel sharper.

## What to actually do

1. If you have fatigue, breathlessness on exertion, hair shedding or falling exercise tolerance, get a full blood count, ferritin and CRP. Ferritin without CRP cannot be interpreted safely.
2. If ferritin is low-ish and CRP is raised, add transferrin saturation before drawing conclusions.
3. Do not start iron before testing, and do not keep taking it indefinitely without follow-up.
4. Men and postmenopausal women with newly found deficiency need a GI work-up, including cancer investigation, plus coeliac screening.
5. Take one dose in the morning on an empty stomach, every other day, with vitamin C, away from tea, coffee, milk and calcium.
6. Reassess at four weeks and continue for around three months after haemoglobin normalises to refill the store.
7. Keep iron tablets locked away from children. This is not a formality.

Iron is one of the few nutrients where speculative supplementation carries real, measurable harm. Deficiency is worth hunting for and worth treating properly — but it starts with a blood test, not with a purchase.
