In short: most children benefit from a daily vitamin D supplement, and for infants it is close to a default. The amount depends on age — **400 IU a day up to 12 months**, **600 IU a day from 1 to 18 years**. A treatment dose for a suspected deficiency, or any special situation, is set by a pediatrician, not a calculator.

## Does a healthy child need it?

For infants, essentially yes. Vitamin D reaches the body from two sources, and both are weak in a child. Breast milk contains very little, so a breastfed baby gets almost none from feeding — which is why the AAP advises 400 IU a day from the first days of life, and formula-fed babies need a supplement too until they drink enough fortified formula. Skin synthesis depends on the angle of the sun, so from autumn to spring in temperate latitudes it adds little.

For older children and teens the case is softer: 600 IU a day is the target, and food plus some sun may cover part of it. A supplement closes the gap when it doesn't.

The preventive amounts above sit comfortably under these ceilings, which is why a standard daily dose is safe without blood monitoring. Raising it "just in case" adds no benefit and starts to add risk; the multi-thousand-IU doses used to correct a real deficiency are a separate matter.

<Callout type="warning" title="When it's the doctor's call, not a table">
A treatment dose for a confirmed deficiency, and dosing in prematurity, liver or kidney disease, or with certain medicines, is set by a clinician — from a 25(OH)D blood test, with a follow-up check. The table above is prevention for a healthy child only.
</Callout>

## Form and how to give it

Use **D3 (cholecalciferol)**: it raises and holds blood levels better than D2. For babies, liquid drops are the practical choice — the form matters little for absorption, consistency matters more. Give it **daily, with a meal**, rather than in large weekly doses.
