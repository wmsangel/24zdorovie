NAD+ is a coenzyme without which metabolism does not work, and its level in tissues declines with age. From this came a popular idea: if you raise NAD+ with the precursor supplements NMN or NR, you can slow aging. The idea is elegant and backed by striking experiments in mice — but in humans it is far more modest. Studies confirm the supplements raise NAD+ levels, yet that this actually rejuvenates or extends life is not proven. Let us look at it without the hype.

## Why there is so much noise around NAD+

The logic is simple and therefore appealing. NAD+ is needed for energy production in cells and for enzymes linked to aging and DNA repair. With age there is less of it. So, in theory, restoring the level to a "young" one could push back age-related changes.

In animals, this logic partly works: in mice, precursor supplements improved metabolism and some age-related markers. It is these experiments, amplified by high-profile books and interviews, that created the market for "anti-aging pills".

<Callout type="science" title="Raising a biomarker ≠ getting younger">
  The key sleight of hand in the marketing is to assume that because a supplement raises NAD+, it "rejuvenates". But raising a biomarker is not in itself a clinical benefit. It is at exactly this step that the human evidence runs out.
</Callout>

The human studies so far are small and short. They say one thing confidently: yes, blood NAD+ can be raised. Beyond that comes uncertainty: some works find isolated metabolic signals, but there is no convincing clinical effect — let alone life extension. This is a normal early stage of science, not a finished result.

## Are they worth paying for

NMN and NR supplements are not cheap, and the promises around them noticeably outrun the evidence. Treating them as a self-experiment with full awareness of the uncertainty is a personal choice. But they cannot be presented as a "proven longevity strategy".

The sober benchmark: for the same money and effort, things with a firm evidence base do far more for healthy longevity.

<Callout type="warning" title="Caution">
  The long-term safety of the supplements is not established, and the regulatory status of NMN is ambiguous across countries. The role of NAD metabolism is under investigation, including in cancer, so with cancer risk and chronic illness these supplements should be discussed with a doctor rather than self-prescribed "just in case".
</Callout>

The bottom line: NMN and NAD+ are an interesting and actively studied area, but not a proven pill against aging. For now it is a bet on the future, not a result — and certainly not a replacement for movement, sleep and diet.
