TSH is not a thyroid hormone — it is the command sent to the thyroid. The pituitary gland in the brain makes it to switch the gland up or down, which is why a clinician checks TSH first. The normal range for most adults is about **0.4–4.0 mIU/L**. A high TSH usually means the gland is struggling and is being pushed harder (hypothyroidism); a low TSH means there is too much hormone and stimulation is being dialed down (hyperthyroidism). But one out-of-range number is not a diagnosis: treatment usually begins when TSH stays above 10, when free T4 is clearly low, or when there are real symptoms — not off a single result.

## Why TSH is measured first

The thyroid gland in your neck makes two hormones — thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3) — that set the metabolic pace of almost every tissue. The pituitary controls them through **thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH)**. The loop is simple and inverse: when thyroid hormone in the blood is low, the pituitary raises TSH to drive the gland; when it is high, it lowers TSH.

That feedback loop is exactly why TSH is the most sensitive marker. It moves earlier and more sharply than the thyroid hormones themselves, so the American Thyroid Association recommends starting with it. If TSH is normal, the thyroid is working normally in the vast majority of cases, and no further testing is needed.

An important caveat: **there is no single 'ideal' TSH number**. The upper limit of normal sits between 4.0 and 5.0 mIU/L across labs, and depends on the assay, age, and pregnancy trimester. Neither the ATA nor the Endocrine Society has adopted the fashionable 'narrow' upper limit of 2.5 as normal for everyone. So compare the number on your report with that same lab's interval, not with someone else's result.

## High TSH: hypothyroidism

A raised TSH is the most common finding. It means the pituitary is stimulating the gland hard because hormone is running low. There are two grades:

- **Overt hypothyroidism** — TSH is high and free T4 is low. This is always treated.
- **Subclinical hypothyroidism** — TSH is high but free T4 is still normal. It is common, and a large share of cases with a TSH of 4–10 mIU/L normalize on their own over a few years without any treatment.

The most common cause in iodine-sufficient countries is Hashimoto's thyroiditis, in which the immune system gradually damages the gland.

## Low TSH: hyperthyroidism

A low or suppressed TSH usually means the opposite — there is excess thyroid hormone in the blood, and the pituitary is easing off. This is hyperthyroidism (thyrotoxicosis). The most common cause is Graves' disease (an autoimmune condition), along with nodules that make hormone without control.

A separate case is an **over-large dose of thyroxine tablets**: if someone is already being treated for hypothyroidism and their TSH drops below normal, that is a signal the dose is too high and should be lowered with their clinician. A low TSH in the first trimester of pregnancy is often normal and not, by itself, a cause for alarm.

## When a high TSH is actually treated

This is the question that drives the most confusion — and the most unnecessary prescriptions.

<Callout type="info" title="The treatment threshold for subclinical hypothyroidism">
  In overt hypothyroidism (high TSH + low T4), thyroxine is always started. In the subclinical form, treatment usually begins when TSH stays (on two tests) above <strong>10 mIU/L</strong>. In the gray zone of 4–10 mIU/L the decision is individual and weighs age, marked symptoms, TPO antibodies, pregnancy plans, and other conditions. About 60% of people with a TSH of 4–10 normalize on their own within five years.
</Callout>

Pregnancy is a separate situation with stricter targets: untreated hypothyroidism in the mother affects the baby's development, so both the threshold and the monitoring are tighter and thyroxine is started earlier. If you are pregnant or planning to be, this is a conversation with your clinician first.

Treatment for overt hypothyroidism is synthetic **levothyroxine** (the same T4 hormone), taken daily on an empty stomach, with the dose adjusted by a follow-up TSH every 6–8 weeks until it is stable. Hyperthyroidism is managed differently — with antithyroid drugs, radioactive iodine, or surgery — and that choice belongs to an endocrinologist.

## Why one test is not a diagnosis

TSH is a living number, and several things move it on their own:

- **Time of day.** TSH is higher overnight and early morning, lower in the afternoon. The within-day swing can be meaningful.
- **Acute illness and stress.** During a cold, after surgery, or after heavy exertion the numbers distort — that is not "thyroid disease."
- **Lab variability.** Different assays give slightly different values.

So a borderline result proves nothing on its own. The right next step after a first abnormal value is to **recheck in 6–12 weeks** at the same lab and roughly the same time of day, adding free T4 and, if needed, TPO antibodies.

## Iodine and selenium: are supplements needed

The short answer is no, not without a reason — and this is an important safety point.

<Callout type="warning" title="Iodine: both deficiency and excess harm">
  The thyroid needs iodine, but the relationship is U-shaped: both too little and too much disrupt the gland. Unsupervised iodine (especially high-dose supplements and kelp blends) can trigger or worsen either hypo- or hyperthyroidism, particularly when an autoimmune process is already present. If you live where iodized salt is used, everyday sources are usually enough. Dose and need are decisions for a clinician only.
</Callout>

Selenium is sometimes discussed for autoimmune thyroiditis: some studies show lower TPO antibodies, but the effect on real outcomes and well-being is unconvincing, and the safe upper limit (about 400 mcg/day per NIH) is easy to exceed with supplements. The conclusion is the same: test and diagnose first, then decide on a supplement with a clinician — not the other way around.

Fatigue, weight gain, and hair loss are easy to blame on the thyroid, but they look exactly the same with iron deficiency, anemia, depression, or menopause. That is another reason not to treat "the thyroid" blind — test TSH first and work out what is actually going on.

## What to do in practice

1. **Get a TSH test** — that one test is enough to screen. In the morning, not during an acute illness.
2. **If it is normal** — the thyroid is almost certainly fine and nothing more is needed.
3. **If it is off** — do not panic and do not start treatment yourself: add free T4 (± TPO antibodies) and **recheck in 6–12 weeks**.
4. **Take the results to an endocrinologist.** The decision to treat depends on how persistent the abnormality is, on T4, symptoms, pregnancy, and antibodies — not on a single number.
5. **No iodine or selenium "for prevention"** without a reason.

The thyroid is a rare case in medicine where one cheap test answers almost the whole question. The main mistake here is not that people are under-tested, but that a single borderline number sends them to hormones or iodine. Test TSH, recheck if it is off, and bring that to a clinician — that is the whole correct path.
