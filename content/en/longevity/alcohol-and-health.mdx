The idea that moderate drinking protects the heart persisted for decades in popular culture and even in some official guidance. In recent years the picture has changed: stricter methods showed that the "protective effect" was largely an artefact of study design. Here is what is known today — without moralising and without reassuring half-truths.

## Measuring the dose

The confusion starts with units. What counts is **pure ethanol**, not the volume of the drink.

The low-risk threshold in the combined analysis of 83 prospective studies (Wood et al., Lancet 2018) was about **100 g of pure alcohol per week**. Above that, life expectancy falls in proportion to intake: roughly 100–200 g a week costs around six months of life, while over 350 g costs four to five years.

## Why "a glass of wine is good for you" was a mistake

Observational studies showed a J-shaped curve for decades: moderate drinkers had lower mortality than non-drinkers. The problem lay in who counted as a non-drinker.

<Callout type="science" title="The sick-quitter bias">
  The "does not drink alcohol" group included people who had stopped drinking precisely because of illness — heart failure, cirrhosis, cancer, dependence. Their elevated mortality was attributed to abstinence rather than to the disease that made them stop. When studies began separating never-drinkers from former drinkers, the J-shaped curve flattened considerably.
</Callout>

The second blow came from **Mendelian randomisation** — a method using genetic variants as a natural experiment. Parts of the East Asian population carry ALDH2 and ADH1B variants that determine alcohol tolerance: carriers physiologically drink less, and that distribution is random with respect to lifestyle. The large study by Millwood and colleagues in 500,000 participants showed that the more alcohol genetics predicted, the higher the risk of stroke and hypertension — **linearly, with no protective segment of the curve**.

**Atrial fibrillation** deserves special mention. The link between alcohol and this arrhythmia is consistent enough to have earned its own name — "holiday heart syndrome". A randomised trial showed that abstinence in drinkers with atrial fibrillation substantially reduced episode frequency.

**Breast cancer** warrants a separate note because risk rises from the very lowest intakes: each 10 g of pure alcohol a day is associated with roughly a 7–10 % increase in risk. The mechanism is linked to raised oestrogen levels and the action of acetaldehyde.

## Alcohol, sleep and recovery

The most noticeable everyday effect is on sleep. Alcohol speeds up falling asleep through sedation, which is why people use it as a sleep aid. But as the ethanol is metabolised, the second half of the night brings a rebound: sleep becomes shallow and fragmented, REM is suppressed, heart rate rises and heart rate variability falls. Wearable trackers make this strikingly visible — even two glasses of wine three to four hours before bed noticeably lower recovery scores.

For people who train there is a further effect: alcohol after exercise suppresses muscle protein synthesis and slows glycogen replenishment.

## What to do in practice

A realistic position: total abstinence isn't the only option, and cutting back delivers benefit in proportion.

1. **Count grams of alcohol, not glasses.** A bottle of wine is 70 g — most of the weekly low-risk threshold.
2. **Build in alcohol-free days.** Three or four consecutive days give the liver time to recover and break the habit loop.
3. **Don't drink within 3–4 hours of bed.** It is the cheapest sleep-quality improvement available.
4. **Separate alcohol from emotion.** Drinking to relieve stress and anxiety is the mechanism that reliably escalates intake.
5. **Don't start "for your health".** No current guideline recommends that a non-drinker begin drinking.

<Callout type="danger" title="When to seek professional help">
  Signs of problem drinking: inability to stop at the planned amount, morning drinking, memory blackouts, continuing despite obvious harm, withdrawal symptoms (tremor, sweating, anxiety, tachycardia). Abrupt self-directed withdrawal with physical dependence is dangerous — seizures and delirium are possible — so it should be done under medical supervision.
</Callout>

## The bottom line

The belief that moderate drinking is beneficial did not survive scrutiny by stricter methods: the protective effect proved to be a design artefact, and genetic studies show risk rising linearly. Alcohol is an established carcinogen that raises the risk of several cancers even at low intakes, and simultaneously a driver of hypertension, arrhythmia, liver damage and wrecked sleep. No safe level has been established, but risk is dose-dependent — meaning any reduction pays off. Count grams rather than glasses, build in alcohol-free days, and don't drink before bed.
