At-home beauty devices promise a dermatologist's results at your bathroom mirror. In short: **LED therapy works, but modestly; microcurrent is weak and temporary; facial EMS has almost no evidence.** No device replaces the things with far stronger backing — sun protection and retinoids.

## LED therapy: modest but real

LED devices emit narrow wavelengths of light. **Red (around 630–660 nm) and near-infrared (830–850 nm)** reach the dermis and stimulate fibroblasts — the cells that make collagen. The proposed mechanism is activation of cytochrome c oxidase in mitochondria and increased ATP synthesis.

This is not just marketing: in a 2014 controlled trial, regular red and infrared light moderately improved skin texture, reduced fine lines and raised collagen density measured by ultrasound. A separate line of evidence covers **blue light (around 415 nm)** for acne: it damages the porphyrins of *C. acnes* bacteria, and here too the evidence is moderate.

One caveat: **home masks are weaker than clinical machines**, so the effect is smaller. Only consistency delivers — courses of several weeks, not a one-off before going out.

<Callout type="science" title="What 'moderate evidence' means">
  There are controlled studies and FDA clearances (via the 510(k) route) for specific anti-wrinkle and anti-acne devices. But the samples are small, some work is industry-linked, and the effect size is modest. Treat LED as a working add-on, not a replacement for your core routine.
</Callout>

## Microcurrent: a temporary effect

Microcurrent devices pass a weak current through the skin, often below the threshold of sensation. Makers promise "muscle tone" and a lifted jawline, citing increased ATP production.

Reality is more modest. A few small studies recorded a **temporary lifting effect** lasting hours to days and needing constant repetition. There are no large, high-quality randomized trials. The FDA clearance brands like to cite confirms the device is safe — not that it works. Those are different things.

The verdict: as a ritual before a big evening, maybe. As a way to permanently change your facial contour, there is no evidence.

## Facial EMS: more marketing than data

EMS (electrical muscle stimulation) uses a stronger current that visibly contracts muscles. There is some evidence for skeletal muscles of the body. But transferring that to the face is not valid: **facial muscles are structured and attached differently**, and quality research on facial EMS "sculpting" is almost non-existent. Most of the loud claims in this category rest on nothing but before-and-after ads.

## Safety and contraindications

LED light is generally safe, but you need **eye protection**, and caution is warranted with photosensitizing medication (isotretinoin, some antibiotics) or a tendency to melasma — heat and light can sometimes worsen it.

## What does more for skin than any device

Before investing in a gadget, cover the basics — their evidence base is incomparably stronger:

- **Sun protection.** In a randomized trial, daily sunscreen noticeably slowed photoaging compared with discretionary use.
- **Retinoids** (tretinoin, retinol). Dozens of trials show reduced wrinkles and more even tone and texture.
- **Not smoking.** Smoking independently accelerates skin aging.

Home devices are an add-on to that base, not a substitute. If the base is in place and you want to add to it, LED is the most evidence-based place to start.

## Bottom line

LED therapy is a useful if modest add-on; microcurrent gives a temporary effect; facial EMS still runs on marketing. No device cancels out sunscreen and retinoids. Buy with realistic expectations: this is about "a little better," not "instead of a dermatologist."
