Neither microcurrent nor light therapy is a proven treatment for age-related macular degeneration (AMD), and neither restores vision that is already lost. Of the two, clinic-administered **photobiomodulation** (red and near-infrared light) has the stronger evidence — one industry-sponsored randomized trial showed small vision gains and the FDA authorized a specific device for dry AMD in late 2024 — but the benefit is modest, unconfirmed by independent trials, and came with a safety signal. **Microcurrent stimulation** rests mostly on small, uncontrolled studies and has no regulatory authorization for AMD. The measures that are actually proven to help remain AREDS2 supplements, anti-VEGF injections for wet AMD, and not smoking.

## Light therapy (photobiomodulation): what the trials show

Photobiomodulation (PBM) uses specific wavelengths of red and near-infrared light, delivered in a clinic, aiming to support stressed retinal cells. The main evidence is the **LIGHTSITE III** randomized, sham-controlled trial in dry AMD (about 100 participants). It reported a vision gain of roughly **5–6 letters in the treated group versus 2–3 in the sham group** over its follow-up.

Two caveats matter as much as the headline:

- That difference is **below the ≥15-letter change** regulators usually treat as clinically meaningful — it is a measurable but small effect.
- The 13-month report noted **more conversions to wet AMD** in the treated eyes (about 5% vs 2%), a safety signal that needs longer follow-up.

The trial was **sponsored by the device maker**, enrolled a small sample, and has **not been independently replicated**. A 2024 systematic review of PBM in AMD reached a similarly cautious conclusion.

<Callout type="info" title="FDA authorization is not the same as 'it works'">
  In November 2024 the FDA authorized the clinic-administered Valeda Light Delivery System for dry AMD. Authorization clears a specific medical device for marketing under a set protocol; it does not mean the benefit is large or settled. The American Academy of Ophthalmology's own commentary called for more study rather than endorsing PBM as standard care.
</Callout>

A critical distinction: the evidence is for **one clinic device used by trained staff**, not for consumer red-light panels or LED masks. No home device has been studied or cleared for AMD, and aiming strong light at the retina carries its own risk.

## Microcurrent stimulation: weaker evidence

Microcurrent stimulation (MCS) applies tiny electrical currents, often through the closed eyelid. The published data are **mostly small and uncontrolled**. A frequently cited 2015 report in dry and wet AMD had **no sham group**; it described improvement in about half of dry-AMD eyes, but the authors explicitly urged caution and called for randomized, double-blind trials.

Since then a few sham-controlled trials have been registered or completed, but there is **no peer-reviewed evidence that establishes efficacy**, and **no regulatory authorization** for microcurrent in AMD. Promotional figures such as "78% of eyes improved" circulate online without verifiable primary sources — treat them as marketing, not evidence.

## What is actually proven for AMD

The interventions with real evidence are unglamorous but worth more than either device:

- **AREDS2 supplements** (vitamin C and E, lutein, zeaxanthin, zinc, copper) slow progression in **intermediate and late dry AMD**; a 2024 re-analysis found they also slow the spread of geographic atrophy when it starts away from the centre. They do not help early AMD or prevent it in healthy eyes.
- **Anti-VEGF injections** are the standard treatment for **wet (neovascular) AMD** and can preserve or recover vision when started promptly.
- **Not smoking** is the single biggest modifiable risk factor; blood-pressure control and a diet rich in leafy greens and oily fish support the rest.

## The bottom line

If you have AMD, the honest position as of late 2026 is this: photobiomodulation is a **promising but unproven** option with one small trial and an FDA-authorized clinic device behind it; microcurrent is **further back still**, resting on uncontrolled studies. Neither replaces the proven measures, and neither should be bought as a home gadget on the strength of these labels. Any decision about light or microcurrent therapy belongs with an **ophthalmologist** who knows your type and stage of AMD — this article is information, not medical advice, and not a recommendation to start either therapy.
