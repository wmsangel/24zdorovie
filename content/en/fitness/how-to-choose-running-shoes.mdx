  - q: "Should I match shoes to my foot's pronation?"
    a: "There's no evidence that matching shoes to your pronation type (neutral / stability / motion control) reduces injuries. Large studies, including on military recruits, found no benefit from assigning shoes by foot shape or arch height. It's a nice shop theory, not injury protection."
  - q: "So how do I choose?"
    a: "By comfort. In research, the shoe that simply feels most comfortable is linked to fewer injuries — this is called the 'comfort filter'. Walk or jog in a few pairs and pick the one your foot moves most naturally in, not the one a store scanner recommends."
  - q: "Cushioning: is thicker always better?"
    a: "There's no clear 'better'. Neither maximal cushioning nor minimalism has shown a clear edge in reducing injuries. A thick sole changes the load but doesn't cancel it. Choose by feel and purpose, and switch to minimalist shoes gradually."
  - q: "When should I replace running shoes?"
    a: "A rough guide is around 300–500 miles (500–800 km), but it's a loose estimate, not a hard threshold — the evidence is weak. It's more practical to go by feel: the midsole feels flat, the cushioning is gone, new pressure points appear — and to rotate a second pair."
  - q: "How much room should the shoe have?"
    a: "About a thumb's width (1–1.5 cm) from your longest toe to the end: your foot lengthens and swells while running. Try shoes in the afternoon and in the socks you'll actually run in."

The main thing to know before you buy: **an expensive "pronation match" does not protect you from injury**, and the best-proven criterion is plain comfort. Everything else — fit, cushioning, when to replace — is secondary and solved with a couple of simple rules. Here it is, minus the shop marketing.

## The pronation myth

For decades shops sorted runners by pronation (how far the foot rolls inward) and arch height, prescribing "neutral", "stability" or "motion control" shoes. When this was tested — including large studies on military recruits assigned shoes by foot shape — **no injury reduction was found**. In novice runners, pronation didn't predict injury risk at all. Put simply, the store's foot scanner sells confidence, not protection.

## What works: the comfort filter

The most evidence-based criterion is how **subjectively comfortable** the pair feels. The "preferred movement path" idea: everyone has a natural foot motion, and a shoe that doesn't fight it feels comfortable and is linked to fewer injuries. The practice is simple: try a few pairs, walk and if possible jog, and pick the one your foot feels easiest in — not the "correct" one from a chart.
