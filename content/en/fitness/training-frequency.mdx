People usually ask "how many times a week" hoping for a single number, but the honest answer depends on the goal: general health, muscle growth, endurance and weight loss all call for a different weekly structure. The encouraging news is that the threshold at which most of the health benefit appears is lower than commonly assumed, and returns above it accumulate slowly.

## The health minimum

The 2020 WHO guidelines set out the baseline for adults aged 18–64.

One detail changed in the 2020 revision: **the requirement for bouts of at least 10 minutes was dropped**. All activity counts, including three flights of stairs and a ten-minute walk to the shop. That removes the main psychological barrier — the sense that a session "doesn't count" if it's short.

## Frequency for muscle growth

Here the evidence is clear enough to answer precisely. Schoenfeld's meta-analysis found that with **weekly volume held equal**, training a muscle group twice a week performs at least as well as once, and sometimes better; comparing two versus three times at matched volume showed no meaningful difference.

So frequency is not magic in itself — it is a tool for distributing volume. Twenty sets for chest in a single session is nearly impossible to execute well: fatigue erodes quality by the end. Split into two sessions of ten sets, the same volume is easier to handle and done with heavier loads.

On maintenance specifically: research shows that **muscle already built can be preserved on roughly a third of the volume** required to build it, provided intensity is maintained. That is worth knowing during busy periods: cutting volume is a legitimate strategy, quitting is not.

Cardio and strength combine without trouble if you separate the emphasis. The "interference effect" — reduced strength gains alongside high volumes of endurance work — is real but appears mainly at high cardio volumes. Practical rule: if strength is the priority, do the strength session earlier in the day or on a separate day; if endurance is the priority, reverse it.

## Recovery

Progress happens between sessions, not during them. Muscle protein synthesis stays elevated for roughly 24–48 hours after training; the nervous system takes longer to recover after heavy compound lifts.

<Callout type="warning" title="Signs of under-recovery">
  - Working weights drop across two or three sessions with no clear explanation
  - Morning resting heart rate is consistently 5–10 beats above normal
  - Sleep has become shallow, or falling asleep is harder
  - Motivation to train has evaporated when it used to be reliable
  - Soreness hasn't cleared by the next session
  - More frequent colds, minor injuries and tweaks

  A **deload week** usually resolves it: keep the same session frequency but roughly halve the volume and stop taking sets to failure.
</Callout>

Three recovery factors outweigh any supplement: **sleep** of 7–9 hours, **protein** at 1.6–2.2 g per kg of body weight per day, and **adequate calories**. In a calorie and protein deficit, adding training frequency only makes things worse.

## When time is short

The commonest reason people quit is planning a week they cannot sustain. A realistic minimum beats a perfect plan on paper:

1. **Two 40-minute full-body strength sessions** — squat, hinge, press, row, plank. That fully satisfies the strength recommendation.
2. **Walking instead of dedicated cardio** — 7,000–8,000 steps a day covers a substantial share of the aerobic target.
3. **Short intense blocks** — 10–15 minutes of intervals twice a week make a noticeable contribution to fitness.
4. **The one-session rule.** In a bad week, one session beats zero: detraining starts after roughly two to three weeks of doing nothing.

## The bottom line

The baseline is 150–300 minutes of moderate cardio and at least two strength sessions a week. For muscle growth, weekly volume decides the outcome (10–20 working sets per group), and frequency exists only to distribute that volume without losing quality — two or three times per group is plenty. You can train daily provided hard and easy days alternate, and at the first signs of under-recovery take a deload week rather than pushing through. And remember: keeping your results takes about a third of the work it took to earn them.
