HIIT (high-intensity interval training) alternates short bouts of near-maximal effort with periods of easy recovery. Its main value is time efficiency: a short session can meaningfully improve endurance and cardiometabolic markers. But HIIT also has an overrated side — the "fat-burning" myth. Here is what this format actually delivers, how it differs from easy cardio, and who should be more careful.

## How it works

The idea of HIIT is to pack a strong training stimulus into a short time through intensity. Instead of a steady 40-minute run, you do several short "bursts" of effort with recovery between them. The formats vary: from short 20–30-second sprints to intervals of 1–4 minutes at a pace hard to hold for long.

It is this "hard–easy" contrast that sets HIIT apart from ordinary cardio and makes the session shorter for a comparable effect on fitness.

## What it genuinely delivers

HIIT's strength is well supported: in less time it improves VO2max — a key measure of aerobic fitness — comparably to longer moderate cardio, and in people with cardiometabolic problems it noticeably improves markers. If time is short, it is a sensible way to get a lot of benefit from a brief session.

<Callout type="science" title="The 'afterburn' myth">
  It is often said that after HIIT "calories keep burning for a day". The raised post-exercise expenditure (EPOC) does exist, but its size is usually greatly overstated — a modest add-on, not a standalone fat-burner. For fat, total calorie balance decides, not the cardio format.
</Callout>

## HIIT or easy cardio

Pitting them against each other is a mistake — they serve different purposes.

For most people the best option is not to pick one but to combine: a base of easy volume plus one or two short interval sessions a week.

## Who should be careful and how to start

HIIT is a high load on the heart and vessels. Beginners without a fitness base, people with heart disease, uncontrolled blood pressure, and those recovering from injury should introduce intense intervals gradually and, where there is risk, after consulting a doctor.

A sensible start: first build a base with moderate cardio, then add short intervals — beginning with a small number and moderate intensity, increasing as you adapt.

The bottom line: HIIT is an excellent tool when time is short and a powerful way to raise aerobic fitness. But it is not a magic fat-burner or a replacement for all cardio — it works best as a complement to easy volume, not instead of it.
