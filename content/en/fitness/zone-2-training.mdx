The idea that progress requires training hard feels self-evident — which is precisely why most amateurs stand still for years. Professional endurance athletes spend around 80% of their training time at an intensity that looks embarrassingly easy from the outside. That intensity is zone 2, and talking about it is really talking about why effort and result are far less linear than we would like.

## What happens inside the muscle

Energy for sustained work comes from oxidising fat and carbohydrate inside mitochondria. The more mitochondria you have, and the denser the capillary network delivering oxygen to them, the more work a muscle performs before switching to anaerobic pathways and accumulating lactate.

Holloszy's classic work in the 1980s established that the volume of low and moderate intensity aerobic work directly increases mitochondrial density. That adaptation is slow but foundational: it raises the ceiling for everything else. High-intensity intervals grow VO₂max faster, but they are built on top of that base rather than instead of it.

The second adaptation is metabolic flexibility — the ability to switch between fat and carbohydrate according to demand. People with high oxidative capacity spend less glycogen at the same pace and therefore sustain work for longer.

<Callout type="science" title="Why this is about health, not just sport">
  Cardiorespiratory fitness is among the strongest known predictors of mortality. In a cohort of 122,007 people (Mandsager et al., JAMA Netw Open, 2018), the mortality gap between low and elite fitness exceeded the gap between smokers and non-smokers. Zone 2 is the main way that fitness gets built, which makes it training for healthy lifespan rather than for a medal.
</Callout>

## Finding your zone 2

The laboratory answer is a lactate test: zone 2 ends roughly where blood lactate begins rising steadily above resting values, usually around 2 mmol/L. Three simpler methods work in daily life.

A practical check: if an hour in "zone 2" leaves you tired and wanting to lie down, you were almost certainly in zone 3. Real zone 2 leaves you feeling you could have done the same again.

## Why medium pace is a trap

The natural amateur strategy looks like this: run as much as you can manage, at a pace that feels like training. That pace almost always lands in zone 3 — between the aerobic and lactate thresholds.

The problem with zone 3 is that it loses on both fronts. For accumulating volume it is too fatiguing: recovery is needed afterwards, and weekly work drops. For raising VO₂max it is not intense enough: the adaptive signal is weaker than in zones 4 and 5.

A systematic review comparing polarized against threshold distributions found the polarized model produced better endurance gains at the same or lower total volume. Training easier most of the time is not a compromise — it is the more effective strategy.

## What it looks like in practice

For someone training four hours a week, a workable pattern is three sessions entirely in zone 2 and one with intervals in zones 4 and 5. That comes to about 180 minutes of easy work and 20–25 minutes of hard work inside the fourth session, the rest of which is warm-up and cool-down.

The first weeks are the unpleasant part. In an untrained person genuine zone 2 is often slower than they are willing to accept: hills require walking, and the running pace looks humiliating. This is normal and temporary — as mitochondrial density grows, the same heart rate starts corresponding to a faster and faster pace. That shift, not the number on the scale, is the measurable result.

<Callout type="tip" title="How to know zone 2 is working">
  Take one fixed route and hold one fixed heart rate on it — say 135. Record your pace. After 6–8 weeks the pace at that same heart rate should have improved. It is the most honest and the cheapest progress test there is, and it needs neither a lab nor a race.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

**Starting with intervals.** HIIT raises VO₂max quickly and therefore looks more efficient. But without an aerobic base, recovery between hard sessions suffers, and a plateau or an injury arrives within a couple of months.

**Calling zone 2 the fat-burning zone.** The proportion of energy from fat really is highest there, but absolute calorie expenditure is lower than at high intensity. Weight is decided by the deficit; zone 2 is not the tool for that job.

**Trusting the watch alone.** Sports watches compute zones as a percentage of maximum and estimate that maximum from 220 − age — two sources of error stacked. The talk test corrects both.

**Skipping strength work.** Endurance and strength solve different problems and do not substitute for each other. Muscle mass leaves after forty regardless of how much you run.

## Who benefits most

Anyone who has "been running for a year without progress" — that is nearly always a zone 3 story. Anyone returning after a break or injury: low intensity provides stimulus with minimal mechanical load. People over 50, whose recovery between hard sessions is genuinely slower. And anyone training for health rather than performance: in observational data it is the volume of moderate activity, not its intensity, that maps best onto reduced mortality.

## The bottom line

Zone 2 is the intensity at which you can still speak in sentences, roughly 60–70% of heart rate reserve. It builds mitochondrial density and capillary networks — the base beneath both athletic performance and cardiorespiratory fitness as a health marker.

The working distribution is about 80% of time in zones 1 and 2, about 20% in zones 4 and 5, and zone 3 nearly empty. The returns are slow: a measurable pace shift at the same heart rate appears after 6–8 weeks, and serious remodelling takes months. That, precisely, is why zone 2 works and why so few people stick with it.
