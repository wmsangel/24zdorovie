Triglycerides are the most volatile line on a lipid panel and the most responsive to how you live. Where LDL is largely genetic and shifts only modestly with diet, triglycerides can halve within a couple of months of dropping alcohol and sugary drinks. That makes them an excellent indicator of metabolic state — and it is also why they are so often overlooked by people who look only at "total cholesterol".

## What they are

Triglycerides are the body's main storage form of fat. They arrive with food and are synthesised in the liver from surplus carbohydrate and alcohol, circulate inside very-low-density lipoproteins (VLDL) and chylomicrons, and are then either burned by muscle or deposited in adipose tissue.

On a blood test, triglycerides reflect the balance between intake, synthesis and clearance. A raised level almost always signals either more energy coming in than going out, or impaired insulin sensitivity — insulin normally suppresses fat breakdown and activates lipoprotein lipase, and that mechanism falters when resistance develops.

## Why they matter

Triglycerides were long treated as secondary to LDL. The picture is now more nuanced. Triglycerides themselves do not deposit in artery walls, but the particles that carry them — remnant lipoproteins — are small enough to enter the vessel intima and drive atherosclerosis. High triglycerides also typically travel with low HDL and small dense LDL particles: the atherogenic dyslipidaemia characteristic of metabolic syndrome and type 2 diabetes.

A separate and far more acute issue is **pancreatitis**. Above 10 mmol/L, chylomicrons overload the plasma to the point of triggering acute inflammation of the pancreas. That needs immediate medical care, not a month of dietary adjustment.

Alcohol deserves special mention: in people with already-high triglycerides, even a few drinks a week keep the number elevated, and a single heavy session can cause a sharp spike. At very high levels, complete abstinence is the first and most effective step.

## How to lower them

The encouraging part is that triglycerides respond faster than any other lipid measure — the effect is visible within 4–8 weeks.

One nuance about carbohydrates: cutting them is not uniformly effective. In people with insulin resistance, replacing refined carbohydrates with whole grains and legumes produces a clear improvement. But an abrupt shift to a very low-carbohydrate, high-saturated-fat diet may lower triglycerides while raising LDL — winning on one marker and losing on another. Changing carbohydrate quality is smarter than eliminating carbohydrates.

## Medication

For people at high cardiovascular risk the mainstay is statins, which lower both LDL and triglycerides by 10–30 %. Above 5.6 mmol/L fibrates may be added — their main job is preventing pancreatitis rather than reducing atherosclerotic risk. Prescription icosapent ethyl (highly purified EPA) reduced cardiovascular events in REDUCE-IT among statin-treated patients with high triglycerides, though the contribution of the mineral-oil placebo remains debated.

<Callout type="warning" title="When to seek urgent care">
  A triglyceride level above 10 mmol/L, particularly with upper abdominal pain radiating to the back, nausea and vomiting, may indicate acute pancreatitis. That calls for emergency care, not a diet starting Monday.
</Callout>

## The bottom line

Triglycerides are a fast, honest readout of metabolic state. Optimal is under 1.7 mmol/L; higher values mean either more energy in than out, or impaired insulin sensitivity. The main levers are alcohol, sugar and refined carbohydrates, body weight and aerobic activity — together capable of a 20–50 % reduction within a couple of months. At very high levels the concern shifts from atherosclerosis to pancreatitis, and that requires a doctor.
