Belly fat is discussed mainly as a cosmetic problem, which badly understates the stakes. Two fundamentally different depots sit inside the abdomen: the subcutaneous one you can pinch, and the visceral one wrapped around your internal organs that you cannot. It is the second that tracks with diabetes, hypertension and cardiovascular events, and the second that neither your mirror nor your scale can see.

## Two different fats

Adipose tissue is not a passive warehouse but an endocrine organ. It secretes leptin, adiponectin, interleukin-6, TNF-alpha and dozens of other signalling molecules — and it does so differently depending on where it sits.

**Subcutaneous fat** lies under the skin on hips, buttocks and abdomen. It is relatively inert, serves a storage role in women, and in its gluteofemoral form is even associated with somewhat better metabolic markers.

**Visceral fat** surrounds the liver, intestines and other abdominal organs. It carries a higher density of catecholamine and glucocorticoid receptors, a higher rate of lipolysis and greater secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Most importantly, its venous drainage runs through the portal vein — straight into the liver.

<Callout type="science" title="Why the portal vein decides everything">
  Free fatty acids from subcutaneous depots enter the general circulation and are diluted. From visceral depots they arrive at the liver as a concentrated stream. The liver responds predictably: it steps up production of very-low-density lipoproteins, suppresses gluconeogenesis less effectively, and stores fat inside its own cells. That is how the chain of visceral obesity, insulin resistance, high triglycerides and low HDL — the metabolic syndrome — assembles itself.
</Callout>

A third depot gets less airtime: ectopic fat, stored inside the liver, pancreas, muscle and around the heart. It travels with visceral fat and adds its own contribution — fatty liver is a risk factor for type 2 diabetes independently of body weight.

## Assessing it without a scanner

The gold standard is MRI or CT at the L4–L5 level. In real life a tape measure suffices.

Waist-to-height is convenient because it needs no lookup table: keep your waist under half your height. In meta-analyses it predicts cardiometabolic risk better than BMI and better than absolute waist circumference alone, and since 2022 it has appeared in NICE guidance.

Bioimpedance scales that report a "visceral fat level" from 1 to 59 deserve a separate note. The method estimates body composition from electrical resistance and depends heavily on hydration; the visceral component within it is calculated rather than measured. Useful for tracking a trend, not for drawing conclusions.

## What actually works

The good news: visceral fat leaves first. It is metabolically more active than subcutaneous fat and mobilises more readily, so a 5–10% reduction in body weight shrinks the visceral depot disproportionately — by 20–30%.

Aerobic training deserves elaboration. In the Verheggen meta-analysis, exercise without dietary restriction reduced visceral fat markedly more than the weight loss could explain: participants lost a kilogram or so while their visceral depot shrank by 6–7%. The practical reading matters — even when the scale refuses to move, regular cardio is doing real work.

The second thread is sweetened drinks. In a controlled trial by Stanhope and colleagues, participants spent ten weeks consuming identical calorie loads from beverages sweetened with either glucose or fructose. Body weight rose equally; visceral fat rose only in the fructose group. Fructose is metabolised predominantly in the liver, and that sets it apart from glucose. Exactly the same argument applies to alcohol.

## What does not work

**Targeted exercises.** The idea that crunches burn belly fat is intuitive and wrong. Lipolysis is a systemic, hormonally regulated process; a muscle cannot claim the fat lying over it. Direct experiments with one-sided training confirm it: the working limb does not lose fat faster than the other.

**Belts, wraps and sauna suits.** Water leaves and returns within a day.

**Supplements.** L-carnitine, green tea, CLA, garcinia, raspberry ketones — none of these categories has shown a clinically meaningful effect on the visceral depot. Reported effects are typically fractions of a kilogram and fail to replicate.

**Fasting as such.** Intermittent fasting works precisely to the extent that it creates a calorie deficit; at matched deficits it offers no specific advantage for visceral fat.

## On stress and sleep

The link between stress and belly fat is real but more complicated than the popular version. Chronically elevated cortisol does favour visceral deposition: visceral tissue carries a higher density of glucocorticoid receptors and greater activity of 11β-HSD1, the enzyme that converts inactive cortisone into cortisol inside the fat tissue itself. Cushing's syndrome, with its characteristic fat redistribution, is the textbook illustration.

But in someone without endocrine pathology, cortisol contributes less than behaviour does. Short sleep and chronic stress raise appetite, shift food choices towards calorie-dense options and reduce spontaneous movement. They work mainly through calorie balance — which is exactly why sleep sits on the list of effective measures alongside diet.

## What to do, step by step

Measure your waist in the morning before eating, tape at navel level, without pulling your stomach in. Divide by your height in centimetres. Under 0.5 means the task is simply keeping it that way.

Above it, start with the two changes that return the most per unit of effort: remove calories from drinks (sweetened and alcoholic) and add 150–300 minutes of aerobic activity a week. Then a moderate calorie deficit of 300–500 kcal, no more — rapid weight loss costs muscle.

Remeasure monthly under the same conditions. Three to five centimetres over three months is a good rate. If your waist is shrinking while the scale holds still, everything is going right: your body composition is changing, which is precisely the point.

## The bottom line

Visceral fat is not a cosmetic defect but metabolically active tissue that drains fatty acids and inflammatory molecules straight into the liver. It underpins the chain of insulin resistance, high triglycerides, low HDL and hypertension known as metabolic syndrome.

A tape measure assesses it: keep your waist under half your height. You cannot remove it locally, but it is the first to go in a general deficit, and regular cardio shrinks it even at unchanged body weight. Nothing sold as a fat burner appears anywhere on that list.
