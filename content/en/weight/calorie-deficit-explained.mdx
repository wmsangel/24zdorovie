A calorie deficit is the one non-negotiable mechanism of fat loss: body fat is only spent when the body takes in less energy than it uses. The interesting question was never whether that is true, but how big the gap should be and how anyone sustains it past week three. The working answer is 10–20 percent below maintenance — for most adults, 300 to 600 kcal a day.

## Working out your maintenance calories

The calculation has two stages: your resting burn, then a correction for how you actually live.

### Stage one: resting metabolic rate

Resting metabolism is what your organs cost to run while you do nothing at all. It accounts for 60–70 percent of daily expenditure, which is worth sitting with for a moment — most of your calorie budget is spent before you stand up. The most accurate of the simple equations is Mifflin–St Jeor:

- **Men:** 10 × weight in kg + 6.25 × height in cm − 5 × age + 5
- **Women:** 10 × weight in kg + 6.25 × height in cm − 5 × age − 161

The two halves are identical apart from the constant: add 5 for men, subtract 161 for women.

Worked example. A woman of 35, 72 kg (159 lb), 168 cm (5 ft 6 in): 10 × 72 + 6.25 × 168 − 5 × 35 − 161 = 720 + 1,050 − 175 − 161 = **1,434 kcal**.

If you think in pounds and inches, convert first — divide pounds by 2.2, multiply inches by 2.54 — rather than hunting for an imperial version of the formula.

Almost everyone overrates their own category. Three gym visits a week on top of a desk job and 5,000 steps a day is 1.375, not 1.55. When in doubt, pick the lower one. Adjusting upward once you see real data is trivial; working out why the scale has been stuck for a month is not.

### Stage three: subtract the deficit

Take 15–20 percent off. For the example at a 1.375 multiplier: 1,972 − 15% ≈ **1,675 kcal**.

<Callout type="info" title="The 3,500-calorie rule is an approximation, not a prediction">
  The old arithmetic — 3,500 kcal per pound of fat, 7,700 kcal per kilogram — describes the energy stored in adipose tissue accurately enough. What it does not do is forecast the scale. As you get lighter, everything you do costs less energy, so a fixed 500 kcal daily deficit stops producing a pound a week and the loss curve bends toward a plateau. Energy balance models built from real weight-loss data show this flattening clearly. Expecting linear results from a linear deficit is the single most common source of premature discouragement.
</Callout>

## The floor you should not go below

For women, 1,200 kcal is the conventional lower bound; for men, 1,500 kcal, absent medical supervision. Below that it becomes genuinely difficult to hit protein, vitamin and mineral needs with ordinary food, however carefully you shop.

A second, more individual floor: do not sit below your resting metabolic rate for long stretches. The deficit properly comes out of total expenditure, not out of the number that keeps your organs running.

Very-low-calorie diets under 800 kcal do exist in clinical practice, used for a limited course under supervision in significant obesity, with monitoring and often meal replacements. That is a medical intervention with a defined endpoint, not something to improvise from a podcast.

<Callout type="warning" title="When counting calories is the wrong tool">
  A history of disordered eating makes tracking a common trigger; measure by habits and food quality instead. Caution also applies during pregnancy and breastfeeding, in adolescents, in people managing diabetes on insulin, and in anyone underweight. In these situations weight loss — if it is indicated at all — is planned with a clinician.
</Callout>

## Why a deficit "stops working"

### You are eating more than you recorded

This is the first explanation to consider and usually the correct one. A classic New England Journal of Medicine study looked at people who believed themselves resistant to weight loss and reported eating about 1,200 kcal a day. Objective measurement put actual intake roughly 47 percent higher than reported, while physical activity was overestimated by about half again. The participants were not lying. They were sincerely, systematically wrong.

The usual leaks:

- Cooking oil — a tablespoon is 120 kcal, not "a splash"
- Sauces, dressings and mayonnaise — 150–250 kcal on a plate without effort
- Drinks: lattes, juice, alcohol
- Grazing while cooking, which almost never gets logged
- Weighing food raw and looking up the cooked entry, or the reverse
- Weekends, which quietly stop being recorded around Friday evening

A blunt diagnostic: if your tracker says you have run a 500 kcal deficit for a month and your weight has not moved, the deficit is not real. Physics is not the variable here.

### Expenditure adapts downward

A lighter body costs less to move. Every step, every flight of stairs is cheaper than it was 8 kg ago. On top of that, spontaneous movement falls — people in a deficit fidget less, stand up less, take the shorter route. This component can run to several hundred kilocalories a day and is essentially invisible from the inside.

### Water is masking the fat

Scale weight is not fat. Glycogen binds water at roughly three grams of water per gram of glycogen, so a swing in carbohydrate intake moves the number 1–2 kg (2–4 lb) in either direction within days. Salt, stress, menstrual cycle phase and a hard session the day before all push it around. Only a rolling weekly average shows what is actually happening to your fat mass.

## How to not quit

Falling off the plan is not a character defect. It is the predictable output of a badly designed one. A few principles do most of the work.

### Protein and food volume

Protein produces the strongest satiety of any macronutrient and protects lean tissue while you are in a deficit. Aim for 1.6–1.8 g per kg of target body weight.

<ReadAlso href="/en/nutrition/how-much-protein-per-day" title="How Much Protein Do You Need Per Day?" />

Fibre and water add bulk without calories. A plate of vegetables before the main course is unglamorous and works — it displaces a meaningful share of the meal that follows.

<ReadAlso href="/en/nutrition/fiber-how-much-you-need" title="Fiber: How Much You Need and Where to Get It" />

### Ban nothing outright

Forbidding a food raises its subjective value and turns a binge into a scheduling question rather than a possibility. The practical alternative is to budget for it: 200–300 kcal a day of whatever you actually want does not derail a deficit, and it removes the sense of deprivation that makes people abandon the whole project.

### Sleep

The most underrated variable in the list. Restricted sleep raises ghrelin, lowers leptin and sharply increases the pull of calorie-dense food. People sleeping under six hours eat more while reporting the same subjective sense of control — which is precisely what makes it dangerous.

<ReadAlso href="/en/sleep/sleep-hygiene-basics" title="Sleep Hygiene: 12 Rules That Actually Work" />

### Lift something

Without a training stimulus, a deficit takes muscle along with fat — up to a quarter of the weight lost. Two or three resistance sessions a week, combined with adequate protein, shift the balance toward fat and protect your resting metabolic rate for the maintenance phase that follows.

<ReadAlso href="/en/fitness/strength-training-for-beginners" title="Strength Training for Beginners: Your First 3 Months" />

<Callout type="tip" title="Count in weeks, not days">
  The daily deficit is a bookkeeping abstraction. Your body settles accounts over longer intervals. If Wednesday ran 800 kcal over, spreading that across the rest of the week is about 130 kcal a day — imperceptible. This single reframe dissolves the "day's ruined, I'll start Monday" reflex that converts one large dinner into a lost week, and it is probably worth more than any food swap on this page.
</Callout>

### Diet breaks

On a long weight-loss run, spending one or two weeks at maintenance every 8–12 weeks is worth building into the plan. It lowers the psychological load and partially restores the hormonal picture. Such breaks do not harm the final outcome, and they measurably improve the odds of reaching it rather than abandoning the attempt in month four.

## Tracking progress properly

Weigh daily under identical conditions — morning, after the bathroom, before eating — and use the weekly average. Compare week to week, never day to day. Individual mornings are noise wearing the costume of data.

Add slower-moving measures: waist circumference every two weeks, photographs monthly in the same light and clothing, and how your clothes fit. In people who train, the scale can sit still for weeks while body composition changes visibly.

Target pace is 0.5–1 percent of body weight per week. At 80 kg (176 lb) that is 400–800 g, roughly 1–1.75 lb. Slower is perfectly acceptable. Consistently faster over months is a signal to shrink the deficit.

## What to do in practice

1. Run the equation, apply your activity multiplier, subtract 15–20 percent.
2. Weigh everything for two weeks. This calibrates your eye — it is not a life sentence.
3. Set a protein target of 1.6–1.8 g per kg of goal weight and let the remaining calories fall where they like.
4. Track the weekly average, not the daily number.
5. Recalculate maintenance every 5 kg (11 lb) lost.
6. Plan the exit before you need it: return to maintenance gradually, adding 100–150 kcal a week.

A calorie deficit is not a diet with a start and a finish. It is a temporary state you need an exit strategy from. A regimen you would not be willing to run for six months is unlikely to produce a durable result, however precise the arithmetic behind it.

Point six is the one that decides whether any of this lasts, and it is a separate skill rather than a longer version of the deficit. What people who keep the weight off actually do — and what the biology pushes back with — is worth reading before you finish losing, not after.
