Cheat meals and refeeds are often sold as a way to "trick" a diet and boost metabolism. The reality is more modest: one large meal does not reset metabolism — the weekly calorie balance decides everything. That said, a planned refeed and a diet break do have a point — a psychological and adaptive one, rather than "hormonal". Here is where the benefit is and where the myth is, and what actually helps you get through a long diet.

## Three different concepts

They are often confused, but the difference is fundamental.

- **Cheat meal** — a spontaneous, off-plan meal, usually large and followed by guilt. Not built into a strategy — more likely to break it.
- **Refeed** — a planned, controlled increase in calories (mainly carbs) for a day or two within a diet. Part of the plan, not a departure from it.
- **Diet break** — a few days at maintenance calories, a deliberate pause in the deficit.

The difference is less about the plate than the approach: a refeed and a break are planned; a cheat meal is not.

## The 'metabolism boost' myth

The industry's main promise is that a big meal "resets" metabolism and leptin, and fat starts coming off again. In reality the effect exists but is tiny. Yes, with overfeeding (especially on carbs) leptin rises briefly and expenditure ticks up a little. But this is a short, modest shift that does not overturn the main fact: weight loss is set by total calorie balance, not by a single meal.

<Callout type="science" title="What decides the outcome">
  One cheat meal will neither make nor break your result — the average balance over weeks does. The problem is not the "cheat" food itself but that a spontaneous lapse often wipes out the whole week's deficit and starts a cycle of guilt.
</Callout>

## Where a refeed and a break genuinely help

The real benefit is not in hormones but in two things: psychological respite and adaptation. A long deficit is tiring and lowers daily expenditure (metabolic adaptation). Planned breaks give you room to breathe, which helps you not to lapse, and may slightly soften that slowdown.

## What is more durable

For most people it is not "endure, then lapse" that wins, but a flexible approach. When there is no "forbidden" food, only a balance — say 80% dietary staples and 20% for pleasure — the broken-restraint-and-overeating effect does not appear. In studies, flexible restraint is linked to better weight control than rigid dieting with regular lapses.

The bottom line: a cheat meal will not boost metabolism, and a planned refeed helps not through "hormones" but by helping you go the distance. The most reliable strategy is not heroic endurance rewarded with a lapse, but a diet in which your favourite food simply fits into the plan.
