## How the conversion works

The difference between mmol/L and mg/dL is not in the test but in what is being counted. **mmol/L** is molar concentration: how many molecules of a substance are in a litre. **mg/dL** is mass concentration: how many milligrams are in a decilitre. You convert between them using the molar mass of the substance.

The conversion factor equals molar mass ÷ 10, which is where the familiar numbers come from:

Total, LDL and HDL cholesterol share one factor — it is the same molecule, differing only in the lipoprotein fraction it is measured in.

<Callout type="info" title="Why countries use different units">
  Molar concentration (mmol/L) reflects the biochemistry more directly — molecules react, not milligrams — and belongs to the SI system used by Europe and most of the world. The US stayed on mass concentration (mg/dL) for historical reasons. That is why US articles, risk calculators and reference ranges often need converting.
</Callout>

## Glucose and HbA1c

Glucose uses a factor of 18.02. Fasting thresholds: normal 3.9–5.5 mmol/L (70–99 mg/dL), prediabetes 5.6–6.9, diabetes 7.0 and above on two tests.

HbA1c is a special case: it is not mmol/L and mg/dL but **percent (NGSP)** and **mmol/mol (IFCC)**. When you select HbA1c, the converter also shows **eAG** — the ~3-month average glucose your percentage corresponds to.

## One caveat

Unit conversion is arithmetic. It does not turn a number into a diagnosis or replace a clinician's reading. Lab reference ranges differ slightly, so always compare your result with the reference values printed on your own report — they are calibrated to that lab's method.
