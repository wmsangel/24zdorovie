  - q: "Can I catch up on sleep at the weekend?"

  - q: "How long should it take to fall asleep?"
    a: "10–20 minutes. Consistently under five usually signals accumulated sleep debt rather than excellent sleep. Consistently over 30, if it affects your day, is the threshold at which insomnia becomes a reasonable conversation. The calculator folds your own figure into the bedtime it suggests."
  - q: "How long should a nap be?"
    a: "20 minutes or 90. A twenty-minute nap does not reach deep stages and leaves no inertia. Ninety minutes is a full cycle, so you wake from light sleep again. The worst option is 40–60 minutes, which lands your alarm squarely in slow-wave sleep. Avoid napping after 3 p.m. — it eats the sleep pressure you need by evening."
  - q: "What matters more, bedtime or consistency?"

## How the calculation works

From the wake time you set, the calculator counts whole 90-minute cycles backwards and adds your time to fall asleep. Six cycles make nine hours of sleep, five make seven and a half, four make six.

Beside each option it shows what most sleep calculators hide: the window that option becomes if your cycle is 70 or 120 minutes rather than 90. For five cycles that window spans nearly two and a half hours — not a defect in the arithmetic but real biological variation.

<Callout type="warning" title="What the 90-minute rule leaves out">
  Cycles are not uniform across the night. Deep slow-wave sleep dominates the first half, while cycles towards morning lengthen and fill with REM. On top of that, cycle length in the same person shifts from night to night with sleep debt, alcohol, bedroom temperature and a dozen other things. Any sleep calculator gives you an orientation, not a timetable.
</Callout>

## What to do with the margin of error

The practical conclusion is simple: never sacrifice duration to land between cycles.

If the choice is seven and a half hours "on cycle" against eight and a quarter "off", take the eight and a quarter. Total sleep duration affects how you feel and how you age far more than which stage your alarm caught. Sleep inertia clears in 15–30 minutes; sleep debt does not clear at all.

Sleep debt accumulates, and the worst part is that you stop noticing. In Van Dongen's experiment, participants slept six hours a night for two weeks; by the end their attention scores had fallen to roughly the level of people kept awake for two consecutive days. Their own ratings of sleepiness, meanwhile, stayed moderate and barely rose.

This is why "I've adapted to five hours" almost always means not adaptation but the loss of any ability to detect the deficit.

## Catching up at the weekend

It works partially. In the Depner study, participants who had been sleep-restricted for a week were allowed unlimited recovery sleep at the weekend: subjective alertness recovered, insulin sensitivity did not. Worse, once the working week resumed, the recovery-sleep group's metabolic markers were no better than those of people who simply kept sleeping short.

The second effect is the clock shift. Waking at 11 on Sunday is the equivalent of flying three time zones west: you are not sleepy on Sunday evening and you struggle on Monday morning. That regular gap between work-day and free-day schedules is social jetlag, and it is independently associated with obesity, metabolic disturbance and depression.

## What matters more than the calculator

A constant wake-up time. It stabilises circadian rhythms more than any cycle arithmetic and keeps working even when your bedtime drifts. If you make one change, make it this one.

Morning light. Fifteen minutes outdoors in the first hour after waking shifts your clock earlier more reliably than any supplement.

And the absence of compensations: if fitting your schedule requires dozing on the commute every day, the schedule is wrong — not your cycle maths.
