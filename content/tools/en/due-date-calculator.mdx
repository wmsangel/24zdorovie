## How the due date is calculated

The basis is **Naegele's rule**: add 280 days — 40 weeks — to the first day of your last period. To do it in your head: **subtract 3 months and add 7 days** to the last period date.

The rule assumes a regular **28-day cycle**. If your cycle is longer or shorter, ovulation happens later or earlier, so the calculator shifts the due date by the difference for a closer estimate.

<Callout type="info" title="Gestational weeks vs from conception">
  In obstetrics, pregnancy is dated from the first day of the last period, not from conception. Conception happens about 2 weeks later, so the "gestational" age is always about 2 weeks ahead of the "embryonic" age. When people say "40 weeks," they mean gestational weeks.
</Callout>

## Why it is an estimate

Only **about 4%** of babies are born exactly on the due date. A span of **37 to 42 weeks** is considered normal — a few weeks either way is normal variation, not a problem. Length is influenced by age, number of previous births and individual factors.

The most accurate dating comes from a **first-trimester ultrasound** (before 13–14 weeks) by fetal size. If it differs from the period-based date by more than a few days, the doctor uses the ultrasound date.

## When to trust the estimate with caution

With an **irregular cycle**, ovulation shifts unpredictably and period-based dating becomes rough. The same applies to a cycle much shorter or longer than average. In those cases ultrasound sets the due date and gestational age more reliably, and the calculator is only a guide.

This tool does not replace medical care: it estimates dates but does not monitor the pregnancy.
