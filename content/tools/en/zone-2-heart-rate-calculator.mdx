## What Zone 2 is

Zone 2 is the **60–70% band** of a five-zone heart-rate model — easy aerobic effort you can sustain for a long time. It sits above a gentle warm-up (Zone 1) and below the "comfortably hard" Zone 3. Physiologically it is where your body builds its aerobic base: more mitochondria, more capillaries, better fat use — and it does this with little fatigue, which is why you can accumulate a lot of it.

The calculator above turns your age (and, if you add it, your resting pulse) into a Zone 2 range. The same 60–70% boundaries match our full [heart rate zones calculator](/en/tools/heart-rate-zones-calculator), so the two agree.

## How the range is worked out

Two steps:

1. **Maximum heart rate** — estimated from age with the Tanaka formula, **208 − 0.7 × age** (and the Gulati formula for women, 206 − 0.88 × age). These are more accurate than the old "220 − age".
2. **The 60–70% band** — by *percentage of maximum* (0.6–0.7 × max) or, better, by the **Karvonen** method, which adds your resting pulse: resting + 0.6–0.7 × (max − resting).

Karvonen is more personal because resting heart rate varies by 30+ beats between people; a fit person with a resting pulse of 45 and someone at 75 have a very different "70% of max". Add your resting pulse above to switch to it.

## Why most of your training belongs here

Endurance research keeps landing on the same pattern: the athletes who improve most spend **most of their volume easy and a small share hard** — the polarized model, roughly 80% in the first two zones and 20% in the fourth and fifth. The classic amateur error is the opposite: nearly every session in Zone 3, too hard to recover from and too easy to drive big adaptations.

<Callout type="tip" title="The talk test beats the watch">
  In Zone 2 you can speak in full sentences but not sing. If you are breathing too hard to talk comfortably, you have drifted up into Zone 3 — ease off. For most people Zone 2 feels slower than expected.
</Callout>

## When the formula does not apply

Age-based estimates assume an ordinary heart-rate response. **Beta blockers, some antiarrhythmics and blood-pressure drugs lower both resting and maximum heart rate**, and arrhythmia or a pacemaker change the picture entirely. In those cases your zones should come from a clinical exercise test, not a formula — and this calculator is only a guide, not medical advice.
