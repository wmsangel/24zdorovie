## How the calculation works

The calculator produces four different numbers, and they answer four different questions.

**Body mass index** is weight divided by height in metres squared. It answers "how much of me is there relative to my height". Quetelet devised it in 1832 as a statistical tool for describing populations, and it was never meant as an individual measure of health.

**Waist circumference** answers where the fat actually sits. The absolute thresholds are 94 and 102 cm in men, 80 and 88 cm in women.

**Waist-to-height ratio** is the same waist adjusted for height: 90 cm means something different on a 195 cm frame and on a 160 cm one. The threshold is identical for everyone and easy to remember — keep your waist under half your height.

**Body fat from circumferences** is estimated with the US Navy method from neck, waist and, for women, hips. It is the only one of the four that tries to say what those kilograms are made of.

<Callout type="info" title="Why waist outranks BMI in the verdict">
  As soon as you enter a waist measurement, the calculator builds its verdict around it. The reason is simple: fat under the skin of your hips and fat wrapped around your liver and intestines behave differently. The second kind is metabolically active, releases free fatty acids straight into the portal vein and drives insulin resistance. BMI cannot distinguish them at all.
</Callout>

## Where BMI fails

It errs in both directions, and both matter clinically.

**Overstating risk in muscular people.** Muscle is denser than fat. A rugby player, or anyone three years into serious lifting, reaches a BMI of 27–29 at 12% body fat. The formula files them as overweight while they are metabolically healthier than most people at 22.

**Understating it in older and sedentary people.** After 60, muscle leaves and fat takes its place. The weight holds, the BMI holds, the composition changes entirely. There is a name for this — sarcopenic obesity — and it carries a worse prognosis than obesity alone.

Then there is a third group: normal weight, large belly. They are described as metabolically obese normal weight — BMI 23, waist 96 cm, insulin resistance and triglycerides in the range you would expect at obesity. BMI never sees them. A tape measure sees them immediately.

## How to measure properly

Accuracy of the circumferences decides everything: a 2 cm error at the waist shifts the estimated body fat by roughly a percentage point.

## What to do with the result

If everything sits in the healthy range: nothing. A stable weight and a waist that does not creep up year on year is itself a decent marker.

If the numbers are outside it, the direction is more useful than the value. A waist that dropped 4 cm over three months at the same body weight beats losing 4 kg of unspecified origin. That is why the calculator also shows fat-free mass index — it tells you how much muscle sits beneath the fat and stops you mistaking wasting for fat loss.

A scale that refuses to move while your waist shrinks is the normal course of events in the first months of resistance training, not a reason to stop.

## What the calculator does not know

It does not know your age in the sense of body composition — a BMI of 24 means different amounts of muscle at 25 and at 70. It knows nothing of ethnicity beyond the coarse Asian adjustment. It does not apply in pregnancy, in children and adolescents (who need age-specific percentile curves), in strength athletes, or in anyone with oedema or ascites, where retained fluid counts as body mass.

Above all: none of the four numbers diagnoses anything. Obesity is a clinical diagnosis, made alongside blood pressure, glucose, lipids and history — never from a single figure.
